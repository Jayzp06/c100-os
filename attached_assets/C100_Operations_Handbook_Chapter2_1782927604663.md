# FVSU Collegiate 100 Operations Handbook

## Chapter 2: Membership Operations

*Status: Draft for review. Builds on Chapter 1: Organizational Mission, Leadership Philosophy, and Operational Principles (Version 1.0, approved).*

---

### 1. Purpose

Membership Operations exists to answer one question: how does this chapter turn a college student's first moment of interest into a lifelong contributor to the mission described in Chapter 1?

This chapter does not define membership as *a student who joins Collegiate 100.* It defines membership as *a student progressing through a leadership development journey* — one that begins before they ever submit an application and does not end at graduation, only changes form. Every stage of that journey, documented below, exists to move a member further along that development curve, not simply to keep them on a roster.

Recruitment fills the front of this system. It is not the system itself. A chapter that recruits well but develops poorly will have a membership number that looks healthy and a leadership pipeline that is empty. This chapter is built to prevent that outcome.

---

### 2. Guiding Principles

**Membership is active, not passive.**
A member who pays dues and attends the minimum required meetings has met the chapter's minimum bar, not its actual standard. Active membership means contributing — to a committee, to a mentoring relationship, to the chapter's culture — not simply being present.

**Every member belongs to a committee.**
Committee assignment is not an administrative formality. It is the primary mechanism through which a general member actually contributes to the mission, and the first real test of the leadership development this chapter exists to provide. A member without a committee is a member without a place to grow, and the chapter should treat an unassigned member as an open problem, not a background fact.

**The chapter is a family — and families hold each other accountable.**
Brotherhood and sisterhood are real, intentional outcomes of this chapter, not incidental byproducts of spending time together. Chapter 1 already established that relationships are strengthened, not diminished, by everyone fulfilling their responsibilities. Membership Operations is where that principle gets tested in practice: the same member who is a close friend outside of chapter business is still expected to show up for their committee, and holding them to that standard is an act of respect for them, not a withdrawal of friendship.

**Recruitment is the beginning of membership, not the goal of it.**
A successful recruitment season produces prospective members. It does not, by itself, produce active members, developed leaders, or lasting impact. Treating recruitment as the finish line is the single most common way membership systems quietly fail — the roster grows while the chapter's actual capacity does not.

**Retention is more valuable than recruitment. Development is more valuable than retention. Impact is more valuable than attendance alone.**
These three statements are ranked deliberately. A chapter that recruits twenty new members and retains none has gained nothing. A chapter that retains twenty members who never grow past general-member status has built a roster, not a pipeline. A chapter whose members grow into committee chairs, officers, and mentors — and whose growth shows up as real impact on the youth this chapter serves — has done what this chapter exists to do. Every practice documented in this chapter should be evaluated against that ranking.

**Every member should leave Collegiate 100 more capable than when they joined.**
This is the standard the entire membership system is measured against, from first interview to alumni transition. It applies whether a member becomes President or never holds a title at all — capability, not title, is the measure.

---

### 3. Scope

**This chapter covers:** the complete membership lifecycle — recruitment, application, selection, onboarding, committee assignment, active development, leadership progression, and the transition to alumni or Sponsoring Chapter status — along with the roles responsible for each stage and the standards members are expected to meet throughout.

**This chapter does not cover:** the internal operations of individual committees once a member is assigned to one (see Chapter 6: Committee Operations), the mechanics of event planning and execution (see Chapter 7: Event Operations), officer election procedures beyond the point of eligibility (see Chapter 5: Executive Operations), or chapter finances, including dues collection mechanics (see Chapter 9: Financial Operations). Where this chapter references those areas, it is only to mark the handoff point — the detail itself lives in its own chapter.

---

### 4. Roles & Responsibilities

**Membership Director** — steward of the membership lifecycle, responsible for ensuring prospective members become engaged members, engaged members become leaders, and leaders become lifelong ambassadors of Collegiate 100. Owns recruitment, vetting, selection, onboarding, and integration of new members into chapter culture; owns retention as an ongoing responsibility, not a one-time onboarding task; and reports directly to the President as the chapter's primary leader for membership development. The Membership Director works closely with the Chief of Staff to monitor member engagement and identify members who may need additional support, but operational ownership of the membership system remains with the Membership Director. *(Current Practice: this function is held by the constitutionally elected Director of Membership. Future Standard: retitled Membership Director, scope unchanged, integrated more tightly with the Chief of Staff role once ratified.)*

**President** — holds ultimate accountability for the health of the membership system, consistent with the Chapter 1 principle that the President exists to ensure the chapter succeeds. Does not run recruitment or onboarding personally, but is responsible for noticing and correcting it if the Membership Director's pipeline stalls.

**Vice President** — ensures that committees, once they receive new members, actually integrate them into real work. A member who is assigned to a committee but never given anything to do is a Committee Operations failure surfacing inside the membership system, and the Vice President is accountable for catching it.

**Chief of Staff** — co-owns engagement monitoring with the Membership Director, and owns the communication side of onboarding: making sure new members are actually reached, informed, and included, not just added to a roster.

**Sergeant-at-Arms** — promotes professionalism, accountability, and respectful conduct throughout the membership experience. While primarily responsible for maintaining order during official chapter meetings and proceedings, the Sergeant-at-Arms also works alongside the Parliamentarian to identify recurring behavioral concerns and reinforce the standards expected of Collegiate 100 members.

**Parliamentarian** — serves as the chapter's advisor on parliamentary procedure, constitutional interpretation, and proper meeting conduct. Works closely with the Sergeant-at-Arms to promote orderly meetings and consistent application of chapter policies. The Parliamentarian focuses on governance and procedure, while the Sergeant-at-Arms focuses on maintaining professionalism and order.

**Committee Chairs** — the first line of member development. A general member's experience of "being an active member" is shaped more by their Committee Chair than by any other officer, since that relationship is the most frequent and most direct. Committee Chairs are responsible for integrating new members quickly and for identifying members ready for more responsibility.

**Secretary** — maintains the official membership records, chapter roster, contact information, participation history, official correspondence, and executive administrative documentation.

**Advisor** — participates in interviews and selection per the National Collegiate 100 Policy Manual, leads the mandatory Start-of-Year Session, and serves as a continuity check on the membership system across officer generations — noticing patterns (a recurring onboarding complaint, a committee that consistently loses members) that any single Executive Board might miss because they only see one year of it.

**Members** — are not passive recipients of this system. Each member is responsible for their own committee participation, their own professional conduct, and — as they progress — for mentoring the members who come after them.

### 5. Operational Workflow: The Membership Lifecycle

The lifecycle below extends the model provided in the design brief for this chapter, with two additions worth explaining before the workflow itself: a **New Member Period** inserted between Selection and Orientation, and **Committee Chair** made an explicit stage rather than folded into "Leadership Development" generically.

Both additions close gaps identified earlier in this handbook's development. The chapter's Constitution already establishes a 30-day New Member probationary period with its own internal officers (President, Vice President, Secretary, and Treasurer of New Members) — that structure exists today and cannot be skipped over in a chapter meant to document the chapter as it actually runs. Making Committee Chair an explicit lifecycle stage aligns this chapter with the leadership pipeline already established as the chapter's operating model: General Member → Committee Member → Committee Chair → Executive Officer → Alumni. Folding "Committee Chair" into a vague "Leadership Development" step would blur the exact transition this handbook is built to make visible.

```
Prospective Student
        ↓
Recruitment Contact  (Membership Director, supported by all members)
        ↓
Interest
        ↓
Application
        ↓
Interview & Vetting  (Membership Director + Advisor)
        ↓
Selection
        ↓
New Member Period  (≈30 days; leadership apprenticeship with own temporary officers, per Constitution Art. XII)
        ↓
Orientation & Induction  (Advisor-led Start-of-Year content + formal induction)
        ↓
Committee Assignment
        ↓
Active Member  (committee contributor)
        ↓
Committee Leadership Development  (informal mentoring within committee)
        ↓
Committee Chair
        ↓
Officer Eligibility Threshold  (GPA and tenure requirements met)
        ↓
Executive Officer Service
        ↓
Mentorship of New Members / Succession Preparation
        ↓
Graduation
        ↓
Transition to Alumni Network / 100 Black Men of America Eligibility
```

**Stage notes:**

- **Recruitment Contact** happens during the National-defined intake periods (September and February). *(Current Practice, set by the National Collegiate 100 Policy Manual — not a chapter-level choice.)*
- **New Member Period** is the chapter's first structured leadership development experience — not merely a probationary formality, and not a separate organization operating alongside the chapter. The incoming new member class appoints its own temporary New Member Officers — President, Vice President, Secretary, Treasurer, and any additional positions established by the Constitution or chapter policy. These positions exist for developmental purposes, not independent governance, and each is mentored directly by its corresponding standing officer for the duration of the period:
  - New Member President → President
  - New Member Vice President → Vice President
  - New Member Secretary → Secretary
  - New Member Treasurer → Treasurer
  - Any additional temporary position → whichever Executive Officer's responsibilities most closely align

  Throughout the period, New Member Officers receive direct guidance, observe chapter leadership in practice, and practice organizational leadership within their own class — this is a leadership apprenticeship built into onboarding, not a credential. Before induction, the New Member Executive Board is responsible for organizing and executing at least one class-led initiative or event, and for delivering a **Lessons Learned Presentation** to the standing Executive Board at the conclusion of the New Member Period, addressing: what went well, what challenged the team, what they learned about leadership, and what advice they would give the next incoming class. This reinforces reflection as a leadership practice from a member's very first month in the chapter, and produces institutional knowledge before that member has ever held a standing officer role — consistent with Chapter 1's principle that institutional knowledge belongs to the chapter, not to individual officers. At the conclusion of the period, authority transfers fully to the standing Executive Board; the leadership experience gained becomes part of each member's ongoing development record and directly seeds the Committee Leadership Development stage that follows.
  This officer-to-officer mentoring model introduces new members to the chapter's leadership culture while building relationships with the Executive Board long before future officer elections.
- **Officer Eligibility Threshold** reflects the Constitution's distinct GPA standards: a 2.0 minimum for general membership, and a 2.7 minimum for Executive Board eligibility. This chapter treats that distinction as a feature, not an inconsistency to resolve — general membership and leadership eligibility are different bars by design.
- **Mentorship of New Members** is listed as its own stage deliberately: a member who has reached this point in the pipeline is expected to be actively developing the members behind them, not simply occupying a senior position. This is the lifecycle's version of Chapter 1's "leaders develop leaders" principle made concrete.

### 6. Standard Practices

- **Committee assignment** should happen within two weeks of Induction — a new member who finishes orientation without a committee assignment is at the highest risk of disengaging before ever becoming active.
- **Attendance** follows the Constitution's standing policy: two excused absences per semester, with advance notice to the Secretary required whenever an absence is foreseeable. *(Current Practice — this is a constitutional provision, not a chapter preference, and should not be altered without a formal amendment.)*
- **Participation** requires engagement in at least 75% of chapter initiatives annually to remain in active, voting status, per the Constitution. *(Current Practice.)*
- **Communication** with new and prospective members should run through the channels the Chief of Staff and Membership Director jointly maintain — not through informal, member-to-member outreach alone, so that no prospective or new member's experience depends on which current member happens to reach them first.
- **Professional conduct** for all members is governed by the Code of Ethics established in the Constitution, particularly around interactions with the youth this chapter mentors — this is non-negotiable regardless of a member's tenure or standing within the chapter.
- **Exit conversations** should be conducted with every graduating or departing member, not just officers — this is where the chapter's most honest feedback about the membership system tends to surface.

---

### 7. Success Metrics

Attendance is one visible indicator of engagement. It is not the definition of engagement, and this chapter should never be evaluated primarily against it. The following should be tracked instead:

- **Retention rate** — from New Member Period completion through at least one full academic year.
- **Committee participation rate** — the percentage of active members who can point to specific committee contributions, not just committee assignment.
- **Leadership progression rate** — the percentage of members who move from General Member to Committee Chair, and from Committee Chair to Executive Officer, over a multi-year window. A pipeline with no progression at any stage is a pipeline that isn't functioning, regardless of how full the roster looks.
- **Mentorship activity** — hours and relationships, tracked at both the youth-mentoring level and the internal member-to-member mentoring level described in the lifecycle above.
- **Professional development participation** — attendance and engagement in the career-related sessions the Chapter Advisor is expected to arrange.
- **Officer pipeline health** — whether there are consistently more eligible, developed Committee Chairs than there are open Executive seats each election cycle. Fewer eligible candidates than open seats is an early warning sign for the entire system, not just for that year's election.
- **Community impact** — hours of service and mentoring delivered, connected back to the Four for the Future areas established in Chapter 1.

### 8. Risks & Common Failure Points

- **Passive membership.** Members who meet minimum attendance and pay dues without ever contributing to a committee are the most common and least visible failure mode of this system — they don't trigger any alarm because nothing is technically wrong, but the chapter is not getting what this chapter defines membership to be.
- **Poor onboarding.** A New Member Period or Orientation that feels like a formality rather than an intentional introduction to the mission produces members who never fully connect to why the chapter exists — see Chapter 1's principle that mission should come before mechanics.
- **Committee disengagement.** A committee that goes quiet doesn't just lose programming capacity — it strands every member assigned to it, since committee assignment is this chapter's primary development mechanism. A member on a dormant committee is functionally outside the leadership pipeline even though they remain on the roster.
- **Poor communication.** New and prospective members who receive inconsistent or delayed information disengage before they ever become active — this is a Membership Operations failure even when the underlying cause is a Communication Operations gap.
- **Loss of culture as the chapter scales.** A larger membership makes it easier for individual members to go unnoticed, which directly undermines the "every member should leave more capable than when they joined" standard. Growth in membership numbers should never be treated as success on its own.
- **Single-person dependency in engagement tracking.** If member engagement is only ever monitored by the Membership Director personally, that tracking disappears the moment that officer is overwhelmed, absent, or graduates — this is why engagement monitoring is a shared responsibility with the Chief of Staff, not a Membership Director task alone.

### 9. Future Platform Opportunities

Consistent with Chapter 1's principle that technology should automate administration, not judgment, the following are administrative tasks worth eventual automation — not leadership decisions:

- **Application and interview tracking** — currently manual; a natural first candidate for the Operations Platform given how directly it feeds the rest of the lifecycle.
- **Committee assignment records** — tracking who is assigned where, and for how long, to make disengagement (an unassigned or inactive member) visible rather than something an officer has to notice manually.
- **Attendance logging** — a repetitive administrative task well suited to automation, so long as it remains one input among several success metrics rather than becoming the system's primary measure of engagement.
- **Member profiles** — a persistent record of a member's committee history, mentorship activity, and leadership progression, so a member's development is visible across their entire time in the chapter rather than reconstructed from memory each year.
- **Leadership progression tracking** — surfacing the officer pipeline health metric from Section 7 automatically, rather than requiring a manual count each election cycle.

None of the above should extend into recommending or deciding who gets selected, assigned, or promoted — those remain human judgment calls, consistent with the boundary already established for this handbook.

---

### 10. Continuous Improvement

Each Executive Board should conduct an annual review of the membership system's success metrics from Section 7, alongside exit conversations gathered under Section 6, and use both to identify where current practice has drifted from the standard described throughout this chapter. The New Member Period's Lessons Learned Presentation should be treated as a direct input to this review — each incoming class's reflections are the earliest and most honest signal the chapter gets about how well its leadership development pipeline is actually working, and should shape real changes, not just be filed away. The membership system, more than most systems in this handbook, will show the health of the entire chapter before any other indicator does — a future Executive Board that keeps this system honest is, in practice, keeping the whole chapter honest.
