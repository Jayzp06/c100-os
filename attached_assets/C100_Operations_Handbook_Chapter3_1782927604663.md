# FVSU Collegiate 100 Operations Handbook

## Chapter 3: Executive Operations

*Status: Draft for review. Builds on Chapter 1 (Version 1.0) and Chapter 2: Membership Operations (Version 1.0).*

---

### 1. Purpose

This chapter documents one central idea: **Executive Officers are stewards of organizational systems, not owners of organizational power.** Everything else in this chapter exists to make that idea operational rather than aspirational.

The Executive Board exists to ensure the chapter succeeds — not to individually run every part of it. Executive Officers are not five (or six, or seven) people leading in parallel; they are a single coordinated leadership team, jointly responsible for removing obstacles, developing leaders, maintaining accountability, and making sure every operational system documented elsewhere in this handbook actually functions. An officer who is personally excellent but operating in isolation from the rest of the Executive Board has not fulfilled this chapter's standard, regardless of how much work they've done.

Every officer described here is evaluated by the organizational outcome they produce — a healthy chapter, healthy committees, healthy communication, healthy finances — not by a checklist of administrative tasks completed. That distinction is what separates this chapter from a job description binder.

---

### 2. Guiding Principles

**The Executive Board succeeds together or fails together.**
This is stated first because it should govern how every other principle in this section is read. No officer's success is fully separable from the rest of the board's. A financially healthy chapter with disengaged members has not succeeded. A well-communicated chapter with no money has not succeeded. Success is a property of the whole board, not a sum of five individual scorecards.

**Each officer owns an outcome, not a task list.**

- The **President** ensures the long-term success of the chapter.
- The **Vice President** ensures committees succeed.
- The **Chief of Staff** ensures members remain informed, engaged, and connected.
- The **Secretary** preserves the chapter's organizational memory and manages executive administration.
- The **Treasurer** ensures the financial health of the chapter.

These are not job titles with attached duties. They are outcomes each officer is accountable for, achieved through whatever combination of tasks the situation requires. A Secretary who takes flawless minutes but lets institutional knowledge disappear at graduation has completed tasks without producing the outcome this chapter actually asks for.

**Technology supports leadership. It does not replace it.**
Every tool referenced in this chapter — dashboards, tracking systems, shared calendars — exists to reduce the administrative weight on officers so they have more capacity for the judgment calls only a person can make. No system should be built or adopted that makes a leadership decision on an officer's behalf. Sometimes the right improvement to a process is removing or simplifying unnecessary work rather than adding a tool to manage it.

**Leadership develops leaders.**
An Executive Officer's success is measured in part by who they leave more capable of leading than before — a Committee Chair the Vice President developed into their own future Executive Officer, a New Member Officer the Secretary mentored who now understands how the chapter's records actually work. This extends the same standard Chapter 1 sets for the whole chapter down into how the Executive Board operates day to day.

**Executive Officers exist to remove obstacles, not become them.**
When a decision routes through an officer and slows down rather than moves forward, that is a signal the process needs to change — not a signal that whoever is waiting should simply wait longer. This principle applies to every officer in this chapter, not only the President.

**No officer operates in isolation.**
Isolation is treated here as a risk condition, not a neutral fact about how someone prefers to work. An officer who is not in regular contact with the rest of the Executive Board cannot actually hold up their end of "succeeds together or fails together" — see Section 5's Officer Collaboration model for how this is structurally prevented, not just encouraged.

---

### 3. Scope

**This chapter covers:** how the Executive Board functions as a coordinated system — its structure, how it meets, how it makes decisions, how it holds itself accountable, how officers work together, and the recurring calendar that keeps Executive leadership proactive.

**This chapter does not cover:** the internal operations of individual committees (Chapter 6), the membership lifecycle (Chapter 2), event execution mechanics (Chapter 7), or the detailed financial procedures the Treasurer follows day to day (Chapter 9). Where this chapter references those areas, it's only to show how the Executive Board coordinates and oversees them — not to re-document them.

This chapter also does not attempt to be an exhaustive job description for each office. It documents the outcome each officer owns and the systems through which the Executive Board operates collectively. Task-level detail, where it exists, lives in the chapters that cover that specific operational area.

### 4. Roles & Responsibilities

**Executive Board Structure:**

| Position | Classification | Primary Organizational Outcome |
|----------|----------------|--------------------------------|
| President | Executive Officer | A healthy, sustainable chapter |
| Vice President | Executive Officer | Healthy committees |
| Chief of Staff | Executive Officer | Healthy communication, member engagement, and organizational accountability |
| Secretary | Executive Officer | Organized documentation, executive coordination, meeting records, calendars, and institutional knowledge |
| Treasurer | Executive Officer | Financial sustainability |
| Sergeant-at-Arms | Executive Officer | Professionalism, order, accountability, and integrity during official chapter functions |
| Advisor | Strategic Advisor | Protects organizational continuity, mentors Executive leadership, and serves as the bridge between the Collegiate Chapter, Parent Chapter, and University |
**Distinguishing the surrounding structure:**

## Directors

Directors oversee major operational functions that support the chapter's long-term success while reporting directly to the President.

Current Director positions include:

- Membership Director
- Social Media Director

Directors are responsible for improving their functional area across the entire chapter rather than managing a committee.

They serve as strategic functional leaders rather than Executive Officers.

## Executive Support Officers

Executive Support Officers provide specialized governance, documentation, and advisory support to the Executive Board.

These positions do not supervise members or committees.

Instead, they strengthen the chapter's operations through expertise in a specific area.

Current Executive Support Officers include:

- Parliamentarian
- Historian
- Bylaws Officer

These officers report directly to the President and work collaboratively with the Executive Board whenever their expertise is required.

- **Executive Officers** hold the seats above and carry joint accountability for the whole chapter's operation.
- **Committee Chairs** own execution within their committee's scope, report through the Vice President, and are not Executive Officers unless they also hold an Executive seat — see Chapter 2's leadership pipeline for how a member moves from one to the other.
- **General Members** are not managed by the Executive Board directly in day-to-day terms; that relationship runs through Committee Chairs, per Chapter 2.
- **Advisor** sits outside the Executive Board's decision-making structure by design. Organizational continuity belongs to the chapter, not to any one person — the Advisor's role is to protect, reinforce, and mentor that continuity, not to personally hold it. This distinction matters because it keeps the Advisor's function (see Chapter 1) from being confused with operational control or with the chapter depending on any single individual, including the Advisor.
- **Parent Chapter** (100 Black Men of Macon-Middle Georgia Area, Inc.) holds oversight authority over the chapter as a whole, exercised primarily through the Advisor relationship and required reporting, not through direct participation in Executive Board meetings.
- **University Administration** holds recognition and compliance authority over the chapter as a registered student organization — a different kind of authority than the Parent Chapter's, and one the Executive Board answers to independently.

**Note on the Chief of Staff:** as established earlier in this handbook's development, the Chief of Staff position is not yet a ratified constitutional office. This chapter documents as if fully integrated into the Executive Board's operations, consistent with the future-state model this handbook is built around — but every Executive Board using this chapter should know that the Chief of Staff's authority is presently informal.

### 5. Operational Workflow

**5.1 — Executive Meetings**

*Purpose:* Executive meetings exist to solve problems and coordinate leadership — not to go around the table sharing updates that could have been written down and read individually.

*Frequency:* Weekly, at minimum, with additional meetings called as needed around major decisions or events.

*Attendees:* All Executive Officers. The Advisor is invited and should attend regularly, but the meeting proceeds and can make decisions with or without the Advisor present, since the Advisor is not a voting member of the Executive Board.

*Preparation expectations:* Every officer arrives with a brief status update on their outcome area (not a task list) and any decision they need the board's input on, submitted to whoever holds agenda ownership before the meeting — not raised cold in the room.

*Agenda ownership:* The President sets the agenda, informed by what officers submit in advance. The Secretary distributes it ahead of the meeting and maintains it as the meeting's structural backbone.

*Decision-making process:* See Section 5.2.

*Meeting outputs:* Minutes, an updated action-item log, and any decisions formally recorded — owned by the Secretary, consistent with the Secretary's outcome of preserving organizational memory.

*Action-item tracking:* Every action item leaving a meeting has exactly one owner and a due date. An action item with no named owner is not a valid output of an Executive meeting.

*Follow-up process:* Open action items from the previous meeting are the first thing reviewed at the next one — not a formality, but the mechanism that keeps meetings from becoming a weekly reset instead of a continuation.

**5.2 — Decision-Making Framework**

Not every decision the Executive Board touches needs the same process. This chapter distinguishes five categories:

- **Strategic decisions** (chapter direction, major partnerships, anything that shapes the chapter beyond a single semester) — require full Executive Board discussion, with the President as final decision-maker when consensus isn't reached after genuine discussion. Strategic decisions should never be made unilaterally by the President without that discussion happening first.
- **Operational decisions** (how a system documented elsewhere in this handbook runs day to day) — owned by whichever officer's outcome area the decision falls under, informed by the rest of the board but not requiring full-board consensus.
- **Financial decisions** — follow the approval thresholds documented in Chapter 9, with the Treasurer and President jointly accountable for anything above those thresholds.
- **Committee decisions** — belong to Committee Chairs, per the autonomy boundary established in Chapter 6. The Executive Board's role is oversight and support, not pre-approval of a committee's internal planning.
- **Routine administrative decisions** (scheduling, minor logistics) — made by whichever officer owns that task, without requiring board discussion at all.

The purpose of this framework is clarity without unnecessary bureaucracy: a routine scheduling choice should never require the same process as a strategic partnership decision, and treating them the same slows the chapter down without adding any real safeguard.

**5.3 — Accountability Framework**

Accountability among Executive Officers is leadership support, not punishment. When an officer isn't meeting their outcome, the expected progression is:

1. **Identification** — the gap is named specifically, by whichever officer or the Advisor first notices it.
2. **Conversation** — a direct, private conversation between the officer and whoever raised it, before anything becomes a board-wide issue.
3. **Support** — practical help is offered: reassigning a task, connecting the officer with someone who's solved a similar problem, adjusting a deadline if it's genuinely unreasonable.
4. **Coaching** — if the gap persists, more structured guidance is offered, potentially involving the Advisor, focused on developing the officer's capability rather than simply repeating the expectation.
5. **Follow-up** — a specific, time-bound check-in confirms whether the support and coaching actually closed the gap.
6. **Executive review** — if the gap remains, the full Executive Board discusses it together, with the officer present, in a manner consistent with a Conflict Resolution process rather than a disciplinary hearing.
7. **Formal action** — reserved for situations where the above hasn't worked or where the Code of Ethics or Constitution has been violated; this step follows the Constitution's removal and disciplinary provisions directly and is not something this handbook can define independently of that governing authority.

The purpose of this progression is to make clear that almost every accountability conversation should resolve well before reaching step 6 or 7 — an Executive Board that regularly jumps straight to formal action has skipped the steps that actually build a stronger officer.

**5.4 — Officer Collaboration**

The Executive Board's principle of "no officer operates in isolation" is enforced through specific, standing relationships rather than left to individual initiative:

- **President ↔ Chief of Staff** — the President sets direction; the Chief of Staff makes sure that direction is actually communicated to and understood by the rest of the chapter, and tracks whether it's landing.
- **Vice President ↔ Committee Chairs** — the Vice President develops Committee Chairs and monitors committee health, escalating to the rest of the Executive Board only when a committee needs support beyond what the Vice President can provide directly.
- **Secretary ↔ President** — the Secretary preserves the record the President relies on to lead consistently across a semester, and is often the first person to notice when a decision has drifted from what was actually agreed.
- **Treasurer ↔ President** — joint accountability for any financial commitment above the routine threshold; the Treasurer manages the detail, the President carries the external accountability for it.
- **Membership Director ↔ Chief of Staff** — as established in Chapter 2, shared, ongoing ownership of member engagement monitoring.
- **President ↔ Directors** — The President provides strategic direction while Directors oversee their specialized functional areas and report organizational trends back to the Executive Board.
- **President ↔ Executive Support Officers** — Executive Support Officers provide governance, constitutional guidance, historical preservation, and procedural expertise that strengthens Executive decision-making without assuming operational leadership responsibilities.
- **Parliamentarian ↔ Sergeant-at-Arms** — These positions work collaboratively to preserve professionalism and good governance. The Parliamentarian advises on constitutional interpretation, parliamentary procedure, and proper meeting process. The Sergeant-at-Arms reinforces professionalism, decorum, and respectful conduct before, during, and after official chapter functions. Together they identify recurring behavioral patterns that may require coaching or Executive Board attention.


**5.5 — Executive Calendar**

| Rhythm | Cadence |
|---|---|
| Executive Meeting | Weekly |
| General Body Meeting | Monthly |
| Committee Check-ins | Per Vice President's cadence with each Committee Chair, at minimum monthly |
| Semester Planning | Start of each semester |
| Officer Retreat | Start of each academic year |
| Strategic Planning | Annually, informing the year's Semester Planning sessions |
| Transition Planning | Final month of each officer's term |
| Annual Review | End of each academic year |

This calendar exists to keep Executive leadership proactive rather than reactive — each rhythm above should be scheduled in advance for the full year at the Officer Retreat, not arranged meeting-to-meeting as the year goes on.

### 6. Standard Practices

- Officers arrive at Executive Meetings prepared with a status update tied to their outcome area, not a list of completed tasks.
- Action items are followed up within one week of assignment, regardless of the original due date, so gaps surface early rather than at the deadline.
- Executive-level decisions are documented by the Secretary the same day they're made — not reconstructed from memory at the next meeting.
- Communication between officers defaults to a shared, documented channel rather than one-on-one side conversations, so decisions remain visible to the whole board.
- Every officer completes a self-assessment against their outcome area at the end of each semester, ahead of the Annual Review described in Section 10.
- No officer should hear about a significant change to another officer's area secondhand — this is a direct test of the "no officer operates in isolation" principle, not a courtesy.

### 7. Success Metrics

Officers should not be evaluated solely by the number of tasks completed. The Executive Board as a whole should track:

- **Officer attendance and engagement** at Executive Meetings — a proxy for whether the "succeeds together or fails together" principle is actually being lived out.
- **Committee health**, as reported by the Vice President — the clearest downstream signal of whether Executive leadership is functioning.
- **Action-item completion rate**, particularly items still open after more than one follow-up cycle.
- **Member engagement**, tracked jointly by the Chief of Staff and Membership Director per Chapter 2.
- **Communication effectiveness** — whether information actually reaches committees and members, not just whether it was sent.
- **Financial stability**, per the Treasurer's reporting.
- **Leadership pipeline health** — the same metric introduced in Chapter 2, since a healthy Executive Board should be visibly developing its own eventual replacements.
- **Executive collaboration** — whether the officer-pairing relationships in Section 5.4 are functioning, assessed honestly at the Annual Review rather than assumed.

### 8. Risks & Common Failure Points

- **President becomes a bottleneck.** The most common and most damaging failure mode for any Executive Board — every decision, regardless of category, ends up routed through one person because the Decision-Making Framework in Section 5.2 isn't actually being followed.
- **Vice President manages instead of develops.** A Vice President who solves every committee problem personally, rather than developing Committee Chairs to solve their own, produces short-term stability and long-term fragility — the moment that Vice President graduates, every committee they were personally propping up struggles at once.
- **Chief of Staff becomes an administrative assistant rather than an organizational coordinator.** Because this is a new, still-forming role, there's a real risk that it gets filled with whatever administrative overflow exists rather than the actual coordination and accountability work it's meant to own. This risk is worth naming explicitly given the role's unratified status noted in Section 4 — an undefined role is especially vulnerable to becoming whatever's convenient rather than what it's meant to be.
- **Secretary only records minutes instead of preserving institutional knowledge.** Minutes are an output, not the outcome. A Secretary who produces perfect minutes that no future officer ever actually reads or uses has not achieved the outcome this chapter assigns to the role.
- **Treasurer only tracks money instead of managing financial sustainability.** Tracking is necessary but not sufficient — sustainability requires forward planning, not just accurate record-keeping of what's already happened.
- **Executive Board operates in silos.** Officers who are individually strong but rarely coordinate produce a chapter that looks fine on paper and struggles the moment two systems need to work together — an event that needs both Committee and Financial coordination, for instance.
- **Poor communication.** The single most common root cause behind failures that appear, on the surface, to be about something else entirely.
- **Lack of succession planning.** An Executive Board that doesn't treat Transition Planning (Section 5.5) as a real, scheduled activity effectively resets the chapter's leadership every single year, regardless of how good this handbook is.
- **Accountability applied inconsistently across officers.** If the framework in Section 5.3 is enforced rigorously for some officers and quietly skipped for others — particularly the President, who has no officer above them applying it — trust in the whole accountability system erodes quickly, even if every individual instance seems minor.
- **Decisions made outside of documented Executive process.** Informal, hallway, or side-channel decisions that bypass the framework in Section 5.2 undermine the Secretary's record-keeping and make the whole Accountability Framework unenforceable, since there's no documented decision to hold anyone accountable to.
Unclear boundaries between Executive Officers, Directors, and Executive Support Officers.

As the leadership structure expands, responsibilities can unintentionally overlap.

This handbook intentionally distinguishes organizational outcomes, reporting relationships, and decision-making authority to reduce duplication and confusion.

Future Executive Boards should preserve these distinctions rather than allowing roles to gradually absorb one another's responsibilities.

### 9. Future Platform Opportunities

Consistent with this chapter's principle that technology supports leadership rather than replacing it, the following administrative work is a reasonable candidate for the Operations Platform:

- **Executive dashboards** — a single view of each officer's outcome-area status, reducing the manual work of assembling updates before each Executive Meeting.
- **Action-item tracking** — automated follow-up reminders tied to the one-week review standard in Section 6.
- **Meeting agendas and minutes** — templated generation and distribution, reducing the Secretary's administrative load without touching the judgment involved in what gets discussed.
- **Officer reports** — standardized formats for the status updates referenced in Section 5.1, so preparation time goes toward the content rather than the format.
- **Calendar coordination** — automated scheduling against the Executive Calendar in Section 5.5.
- **Document management** — a persistent, searchable record of Executive decisions, directly supporting the Secretary's institutional-knowledge outcome.
- **Committee reporting** — pulling committee health data (Chapter 6) directly into the Vice President's Executive Meeting preparation.
Technology initiatives should always support the Executive Board's operational effectiveness regardless of whether a dedicated Technology Chair has been appointed.

Technology is a chapter resource—not the responsibility of a single officer.

None of the above should extend into recommending or making a leadership decision — every item here reduces the effort of preparing for or recording a decision, not the effort of making one.

### Leadership Succession

Every Executive Officer should intentionally identify and develop at least one potential successor during their term. This is not a task reserved for the end of the year — succession planning begins early in the academic year, alongside Semester Planning, not after elections are already underway.

This happens through the same relationships already documented in this chapter, given a deliberate purpose: Executive Officers actively mentor Committee Chairs, New Member Officers, and other emerging leaders throughout the year, not only when a vacancy is approaching. Leadership development, as Chapter 1 establishes, is a continuous responsibility of every officer — not an end-of-year activity squeezed in before elections.

This connects directly to the leadership pipeline established in Chapter 2: General Member → Committee Member → Committee Chair → Executive Officer → Alumni. The New Member Officer mentorship model in Chapter 2 — where each temporary officer is paired directly with their standing counterpart — is this pipeline's earliest form of succession planning, and the same principle should continue at every later stage. A Vice President developing a Committee Chair, a Secretary training a New Member Secretary, a Treasurer walking a promising member through the budget process — each of these is succession planning in practice, not a separate initiative layered on top of the officer's actual work.

The objective of this section is straightforward: future elections should produce multiple qualified candidates for each seat, not a scramble to fill a vacancy at the last minute because no one was deliberately prepared. An Executive Board that reaches election season with only one plausible candidate per seat has not failed at elections — it has failed at succession planning months earlier.

### 10. Continuous Improvement

At the end of each academic year, the Executive Board should conduct a structured self-evaluation combining:

- **Officer self-assessments** against each outcome area, gathered before the group discussion so individual reflection isn't shaped by the room.
- **An Executive retreat** dedicated specifically to this review, separate from routine planning retreats.
- **Advisor feedback**, given the Advisor's cross-year continuity perspective established in Chapter 1.
- **Committee Chair feedback** on whether the Vice President relationship and broader Executive Board support actually helped their committees succeed.
- **Member feedback**, gathered through the exit conversations and engagement channels established in Chapter 2.
- **Lessons learned**, documented in the same spirit as the New Member Lessons Learned Presentation from Chapter 2 — reflection as a leadership practice, not just a formality reserved for new members.
- **Transition documentation**, prepared during the Transition Planning period in Section 5.5, so the incoming Executive Board inherits both the systems in this chapter and the specific lessons this year's board learned running them.

The standard for this review is the same one that governs the whole handbook: the Executive Board should leave the chapter stronger than it inherited it — not identical, and not radically remade, but demonstrably, honestly improved.
