# FVSU Collegiate 100 Operations Handbook

## Chapter 4: Committee Operations

*Status: Draft for review. Builds on Chapter 1 (v1.0), Chapter 2 (v1.0), and Chapter 3 (v1.1).*

---

### 1. Purpose

This is not a chapter about committee meetings, and it is not a chapter about event planning. It is a chapter about how this chapter turns its mission into measurable impact. Committees are the operational engine of the organization: the Executive Board provides strategic leadership, but committees are where that leadership actually becomes mentoring relationships, academic support, service projects, and developed leaders.

Every committee should produce a measurable organizational outcome, not simply host activities. Events are one method a committee uses to accomplish its mission — they are never the mission itself. A committee that runs a full calendar of well-attended events but can't point to what changed for the students or community it serves has not succeeded by this chapter's standard, regardless of how busy it looked.

This chapter exists to operationalize a specific piece of Chapter 3: the Vice President's outcome is healthy committees. This chapter defines what "healthy" actually means for a committee and documents how that health gets produced in practice.

---

### 2. Guiding Principles

**Committees own execution.**
Consistent with the autonomy boundary established in Chapter 3's Decision-Making Framework, committee-level decisions belong to Committee Chairs. The Executive Board's role is oversight and support, not pre-approval of a committee's internal planning.

**Committee Chairs develop future leaders.**
A Committee Chair is measured by the leaders they develop, not merely by the events they host. This is the same standard Chapter 3 applies to Executive Officers, extended one level down — leadership development is not reserved for people who already hold a title.

**Initiative is expected. Accountability is expected.** Neither substitutes for the other, and both apply equally to Committee Chairs and Committee Members — a member who takes initiative without following through has not met the standard, and neither has one who follows instructions without ever contributing an idea.

**Oversight should never become micromanagement.**
The Vice President reviews committee health; the Vice President does not run committees personally. The moment Executive oversight starts making decisions a Committee Chair is capable of making, the chapter has recreated the exact bottleneck this handbook was built to eliminate.

**Meetings are working sessions, not information sessions.**
A committee meeting that consists of announcements and nothing else has not used its time — see Section 5.6 for what a working session actually includes.

**Committees should improve the community — not just maintain themselves.**
A committee's first obligation is to the outcome it exists to produce, not to its own continuity. A committee that has stopped producing real impact should be reconsidered, not preserved out of habit — see Chapter 6 (Committee Operations governance, dormancy, and reactivation) for how that's handled structurally.

**Success is measured by impact, not activity.**
Number of meetings held and number of events hosted are not success metrics in this chapter — see Section 7 for what is measured instead.

**Every committee should leave the chapter stronger than it found it, and committee work should remain scalable whether the chapter has 15 members or 150.**
These two principles govern every structural choice documented below, particularly Section 4's Organizational Scaling model.

---

### 3. Scope

**This chapter covers:** the standing committee structure, how committees report into the Executive Board, how members are assigned to committees, how a committee defines and pursues its mission, the full lifecycle an idea travels from identification to archive, and how committee meetings and continuous improvement actually work.

**This chapter does not cover:** the detailed mechanics of individual event execution once a proposal has cleared committee-level planning (Chapter 7), the chapter's broader communication systems beyond committee reporting (Chapter 8), or financial procedures beyond the point where a Treasurer review is triggered (Chapter 9). Where those areas intersect with committee work, this chapter marks the handoff point rather than re-documenting the detail.

### 4. Roles & Responsibilities

**Committee Structure.**

**Standing Committees (Permanent):**
- Mentoring
- Education
- Economic Empowerment
- Leadership Development
- Health & Wellness
- Community Service

**Operational Support Teams (Appointed as Needed):**

Operational Support Teams are temporary, cross-functional teams created by the President and Executive Board to accomplish a specific organizational objective. Examples include:
- Special Events Team
- Conference Planning Team
- Strategic Initiative Team

These examples are illustrative rather than exhaustive. Future Executive Boards may create additional Operational Support Teams whenever a temporary objective falls outside the ongoing mission of the chapter's Standing Committees. Operational Support Teams should exist only for the duration of their assigned objective and should be dissolved upon completion. The purpose of this model is to provide organizational flexibility without expanding the permanent committee structure.

Standing Committees carry the chapter's ongoing mission year-round and are where every member's Primary Committee assignment lives. Operational Support Teams are a different kind of structure entirely — not permanent homes for ongoing work. Future Presidents may establish additional Operational Support Teams as new needs arise, without requiring a constitutional amendment or any restructuring of the chapter's standing committee system. Their purpose is to give the chapter organizational flexibility while protecting the stability of the standing committee structure — committees own ongoing mission areas; teams own temporary objectives.

Committee names may evolve over time as the chapter's priorities shift. The underlying organizational outcomes each committee exists to produce should remain stable even when a name changes.

*Reconciliation note:* the National Collegiate 100 Policy Manual specifies a different six-committee baseline — Mentoring, Education, Health & Wellness, Economic Development, Membership, and Special Programs. This chapter's structure adapts that baseline rather than contradicting it: Mentoring, Education, and Health & Wellness carry forward directly; Economic Empowerment maps to Economic Development; Membership's chair-level function is now owned by the Membership Director role established in Chapter 2, not a standing committee; and Special Programs is now carried by the Operational Support Teams category above — most directly by the Special Events Team — rather than folded permanently into a sixth standing committee, with Community Service and Leadership Development taking that place as standing structures better matched to this chapter's actual, recurring work. Any future Executive Board revising this list should keep this mapping in mind so the chapter's structure doesn't quietly drift out of alignment with what National expects a standing committee to cover.

**Team Leads.**

Every Operational Support Team requires leadership, distinct from the standing Committee Chair structure above. Each team has a **Team Lead**, appointed by the President or Executive Board specifically for that team's objective. The Team Lead is responsible for:
- Coordinating the team's work.
- Delegating responsibilities among team members.
- Communicating progress to Executive leadership.
- Reporting to Executive leadership on a regular basis for the duration of the assignment.
- Dissolving the team upon successful completion of its objective.

A Team Lead is not a permanent officer position. It exists only for the duration of the team's assignment, and carries no standing authority once that assignment concludes — a member who serves as a Team Lead once is not automatically a Team Lead again unless appointed to a new team.

**Committee Reporting Structure.**

```
President
    ↓
Vice President
    ↓
Committee Chairs
    ↓
Committee Members
```

- **President** — removes organizational barriers, provides strategic direction, and approves major initiatives when appropriate. This is the committee-facing slice of the President's broader Chapter 3 outcome (a healthy, sustainable chapter), not a separate responsibility.
- **Vice President** — develops Committee Chairs, reviews committee health, coordinates committee operations, and ensures committees remain aligned with chapter goals. This is the full operational meaning of the Vice President's Chapter 3 outcome: healthy committees.
- **Committee Chair** — leads execution, develops committee members, plans committee initiatives, coordinates committee meetings, and reports progress to the Vice President.
- **Committee Members** — contribute ideas, execute committee work, participate actively, and develop leadership skills through that work.

**Committee Assignment Model.**

Every member belongs to one **Primary Committee** — their leadership home, and the committee through which their development and accountability are primarily tracked. Members may also participate in a **Secondary Committee**, when appropriate, to:
- Support chapter priorities that need cross-committee attention.
- Utilize specialized skills a member has that don't fit neatly under their Primary Committee.
- Encourage interdisciplinary collaboration between committees.
- Address temporary staffing needs.

Secondary Committee participation should never interfere with Primary Committee responsibilities. A member's Primary Committee is where their engagement, development, and accountability are actually measured — Secondary participation is additive, not a substitute, and should never become a way to appear engaged overall while under-contributing to the committee that's actually responsible for that member's development.

**Organizational Scaling.**

Committee assignment should evolve as the chapter's size changes — the goal at every size is meaningful engagement, not maximizing the number of committee memberships a member holds.

- **Small Chapter:** members may serve on multiple committees out of practical necessity, since there may not be enough members to staff every committee at full strength otherwise.
- **Medium Chapter:** one Primary Committee per member, with Secondary Committee participation optional or assigned based on need.
- **Large Chapter:** Primary Committee assignment remains the norm, supplemented by Operational Support Teams — a Special Events Team, Conference Planning Team, Strategic Initiative Team, or others appointed as needed — for work that doesn't fit neatly into a standing committee's ongoing mission. This is the chapter's answer to Special Programs-style work (see the Reconciliation note above) once the chapter is large enough to staff it without pulling capacity from the standing committees.

### 5. Operational Workflow

**5.1 — Committee Mission Framework**

Every committee should define, and periodically revisit, the following:

- **Mission** — what the committee exists to accomplish.
- **Organizational Outcome** — the measurable change the committee's mission produces.
- **Annual Goals** and **Semester Goals** — the committee's mission broken into time-bound targets.
- **Key Initiatives** — the specific work planned to reach those goals.
- **Success Metrics** — see Section 7 for the chapter-wide standard this should align with.
- **Leadership Opportunities** — how the committee develops its members, consistent with Section 2's principle that Committee Chairs are measured by the leaders they develop.
- **Community Impact** — the effect on the youth and community this chapter exists to serve.

Committees should be defined by these measurable outcomes, not by their recurring events. A committee that can only describe itself by listing the events it runs each semester has not yet completed this framework.

**Starting mission statements for the current standing committees** (each should be developed further into the full framework above, not treated as complete on their own):

| Committee | Mission |
|---|---|
| Mentoring | Develop students through meaningful mentoring relationships. Success means students are actually mentored — not simply invited to events. |
| Education | Support academic excellence, intellectual growth, and educational opportunity. |
| Economic Empowerment | Improve financial literacy, entrepreneurship, career readiness, and economic opportunity. |
| Leadership Development | Develop future leaders within the chapter. |
| Health & Wellness | Promote physical, mental, emotional, and social wellness. |
| Community Service | Strengthen the surrounding community through meaningful service. |

**5.2 — Cross-Committee Collaboration**

Healthy committees intentionally collaborate whenever their missions naturally overlap, rather than treating every initiative as belonging exclusively to one committee. This is where the Secondary Committee relationships in Section 4 show up in practice:

| Collaborating Committees | Example Initiative |
|---|---|
| Education + Economic Empowerment | Financial Literacy Workshop |
| Mentoring + Leadership Development | Freshman Leadership Retreat |
| Community Service + Health & Wellness | Community Health Fair |
| Health & Wellness + Mentoring | Mental Wellness Mentorship Series |

The objective is to encourage interdisciplinary thinking while allowing each committee to retain ownership of its own mission — a collaborative initiative should be traceable back to a specific outcome in each participating committee's Mission Framework (Section 5.1), not treated as a one-off event floating outside either committee's actual goals.

**5.3 — Capacity Management**

Before accepting a new initiative, a Committee Chair should evaluate the committee's actual capacity to execute it well — not just its enthusiasm for the idea. This means weighing:
- Current initiatives already in progress.
- Member availability.
- Available resources.
- Semester priorities set during Semester Planning (Chapter 3).
- Execution capacity — whether the committee can do this well, not just do it.

When a committee reaches capacity, the Committee Chair should recommend one of the following, rather than simply overcommitting:
- Cross-committee collaboration (Section 5.2), if a related committee has the capacity this one lacks.
- Creation of an Operational Support Team (Section 4), if the work is temporary and objective-specific enough to justify one.
- Delaying lower-priority initiatives to a future semester.

The goal is sustainable execution, not overcommitment — a committee that says yes to everything and executes little has not served its mission any better than one that said no too often.

**5.4 — Committee Operational Lifecycle**

```
Need Identified
        ↓
Committee Discussion
        ↓
Idea Development
        ↓
Committee Proposal
        ↓
Committee Chair Review
        ↓
Project Owner Assigned  (Chair, Vice Chair, Team Lead,
        ↓                Event Coordinator, or Designated Project Lead)
Vice President Review  ─────┐
        ↓                    │
Executive Awareness          │  visibility, not approval —
        ↓                    │  see note below
Advisor Awareness  ──────────┘
        ↓
Budget Review (if required)
        ↓
Facility Request (if required, also satisfies University submission)
        ↓
Marketing Request
        ↓
Execution
        ↓
After Action Review
        ↓
Lessons Learned
        ↓
Knowledge Archive  ─────▶ feeds back into Section 5.1's Mission Framework
                           at the committee's next planning cycle
```

Three changes from a straightforward idea-to-archive pipeline are worth explaining:

**Project Owner Assigned is a deliberate addition.** Not every initiative should default to the Committee Chair personally leading execution — doing so is exactly the burnout risk named in Section 8. Once the Chair has reviewed and approved a proposal, ownership of execution is explicitly assigned to whoever is best positioned to carry it out: the Chair themselves, a Committee Vice Chair where one exists, a Team Lead if the initiative has been routed to an Operational Support Team, an Event Coordinator, or another designated Project Lead. This makes delegation a visible, accountable step in the lifecycle rather than an informal habit that depends on each Chair's individual instinct to hand work off.

**Vice President Review and Executive Awareness are not the same kind of step, and neither is an approval gate in the way Budget Review or Facility Request are.** Per Chapter 3's Decision-Making Framework, committee decisions belong to Committee Chairs — the Executive Board's role here is oversight and support, not pre-approval. Vice President Review confirms the proposal is ready and aligned with the committee's mission; Executive Awareness simply means the rest of the Executive Board knows what's coming, so no officer is blindsided later. Budget Review and Facility Request, by contrast, are genuine approval gates, because they commit chapter resources or external facility relationships — consistent with the distinction Chapter 3 draws between operational and financial decisions.

**Advisor Awareness is added as its own step, parallel to Executive Awareness.** The Advisor is not an Executive Officer (Chapter 3) and would otherwise have no defined visibility into committee-level planning under this lifecycle, despite the National Collegiate 100 Policy Manual expecting the Chapter Advisor to be informed of all planned programs, projects, and operations executed on or off campus. Without this step, the Advisor's continuity function — protecting and reinforcing organizational continuity, per Chapter 3 — would have no actual mechanism to operate through at the committee level. This closes a gap identified earlier in this handbook's development, where no workflow in the chapter gave the Advisor visibility before a plan was already underway.

**Knowledge Archive is not a dead end.** It feeds directly back into the committee's Mission Framework at the start of its next planning cycle — consistent with the whole handbook's treatment of knowledge as something that has to actively flow back into future planning, not simply accumulate in storage.

**5.5 — Facility Reservation Workflow**

This documents the intended future workflow, built for the eventual Operations Platform but usable manually today:

1. Committee members collaborate on ideas during a working session (Section 5.6).
2. The Committee Chair develops the proposal.
3. A standardized facility request is completed. The Operations Platform should eventually pre-populate recurring information — Advisor, chapter information, contact information — so Committee Chairs only complete event-specific details each time.
4. The Vice President reviews readiness.
5. The Treasurer reviews financial implications, where relevant.
6. The President approves major initiatives, where appropriate, consistent with the President's role in Section 4.
7. The Chief of Staff coordinates communication and organizational timelines around the request.
8. Facility requests must satisfy University submission deadlines — this is a hard external constraint, not a chapter preference, and planning timelines should work backward from it.

> **Design Principle: Administrative work should be minimized. Accountability should not.**
> This is one of the clearest operating principles in this handbook. Reducing paperwork, pre-populating forms, and simplifying process are all worth pursuing — but never as a way to reduce who is answerable for an outcome. Every future Operations Platform feature evaluated against this chapter should be measured against both halves of this sentence, not just the first.

**5.6 — Committee Meetings**

Committee meetings are working sessions, not information sessions. Every meeting should include:
- Mission review
- Planning
- Problem solving
- Leadership development
- Task delegation
- Progress review
- Reflection

Every meeting should end with assigned responsibilities, named owners, deadlines, and clear follow-up expectations — a meeting that ends without all four has not actually produced a working outcome, regardless of how good the discussion was.

### 6. Standard Practices

- **Meeting cadence** — set by each Committee Chair, at minimum aligned with the chapter's monthly General Body rhythm from Chapter 3, with more frequent internal committee meetings as initiatives require.
- **Attendance** — tracked per the chapter-wide standard in Chapter 2; committee-level attendance feeds into that member's overall participation record, not a separate one.
- **Communication** — committee updates flow through the reporting structure in Section 4, and should also reach the Chief of Staff for the chapter-wide communication picture established in Chapter 3.
- **Planning** — semester goals are set at the start of each semester, consistent with the Executive Calendar's Semester Planning rhythm from Chapter 3.
- **Documentation** — every proposal, budget request, and After Action Review is recorded, not just remembered — this is the committee-level instance of the institutional knowledge principle running throughout this handbook.
- **Knowledge capture** — Lessons Learned from Section 5.4 are written down before the committee moves to its next initiative, not reconstructed later from memory.
- **After Action Reviews** — conducted after every major initiative, not only the ones that went poorly; a successful initiative's lessons are just as valuable to capture as a struggling one's.
- **Committee reports** — delivered to the Vice President on a regular, predictable cadence, not only when something goes wrong.
- **Leadership development** — an explicit, standing agenda item for Committee Chairs, not an incidental byproduct of doing the work.
- **Cross-committee collaboration** — actively pursued where a Secondary Committee relationship (Section 4) or the collaboration model in Section 5.2 makes it possible, rather than left to chance.

### 7. Success Metrics

Number of meetings held and number of events hosted are explicitly not the standard here. Instead, evaluate:

- **Mission accomplishment** — against the goals set in each committee's Mission Framework.
- **Leadership development** — members who grew into greater responsibility within or beyond the committee.
- **Community impact** — the measurable effect on the youth and community this chapter serves.
- **Member engagement** — participation quality, not just attendance.
- **Committee participation** — whether members assigned to a committee are actually contributing, per Section 2's "active, not passive" standard from Chapter 2.
- **Innovation** — whether the committee is developing new approaches, not only repeating what worked last year.
- **Execution quality** — whether initiatives that reach Execution in Section 5.4 are actually well-run, not just completed.
- **Knowledge preservation** — whether Lessons Learned and Knowledge Archive entries actually exist and get used at the next planning cycle.
- **Cross-committee collaboration** — actual instances of it, not just the possibility for it.
- **Long-term outcomes** — impact that persists beyond a single semester or event.

### 8. Risks & Common Failure Points

- **Committee Chair burnout.** A Chair who is personally running every part of the committee, rather than delegating and developing members, is on a path to burnout that also stunts every member around them — this is the committee-level version of the Vice President risk named in Chapter 3.
- **Passive committee members.** The most common failure this chapter is built to prevent — see Chapter 2's "membership is active, not passive" principle, now applied at the committee level where it actually gets tested.
- **Mission drift.** A committee that keeps running events because they're familiar, without checking those events against its actual Mission Framework, will drift from its outcome without anyone noticing until the After Action Review makes it obvious — or doesn't, if that review isn't happening.
- **Poor delegation, late planning, poor communication, and poor documentation.** Each of these, individually, tends to look like a minor process gap. In combination, they're usually the real story behind a committee that "just isn't working" without any single dramatic failure.
- **Executive micromanagement.** The direct violation of Section 2's "oversight should never become micromanagement" principle — a Vice President or President who steps into a Committee Chair's execution decisions has recreated the exact bottleneck this handbook exists to eliminate.
- **Weak succession planning.** The committee-level counterpart to the Leadership Succession section in Chapter 3 — a Committee Chair who doesn't intentionally develop a successor leaves their committee exposed the moment they graduate or step down.
- **Ideas never reaching execution.** A lifecycle (Section 5.4) that exists on paper but isn't actually followed produces committees full of good ideas and empty of finished initiatives.
- **Committees becoming event planners instead of mission teams.** The single most important risk in this chapter, and the reason Section 1 opens by explicitly separating events from mission — a committee can look fully active by this failure mode's own standard while completely missing this chapter's actual definition of success.
- **Primary/Secondary Committee ambiguity.** Worth naming explicitly, since it isn't in the original risk list: Secondary Committee participation can quietly become an excuse for under-engagement with a member's actual Primary Committee, or a way for a member to appear broadly involved without being deeply accountable anywhere. Section 4's rule — Secondary should never interfere with Primary — needs active enforcement by the Vice President, not just a stated policy.
- **Uneven committee engagement.** Some committees (historically Mentoring and Community Service, per the pattern already flagged in Chapter 1) tend to attract members and initiatives more easily than others (Economic Empowerment and Health & Wellness typically take more deliberate effort to program well). Left unaddressed, this produces a chapter that looks balanced on the Committee Structure list in Section 4 while being seriously imbalanced in practice.

### 9. Future Platform Opportunities

Consistent with this handbook's standing rule that technology reduces administrative work rather than replacing leadership judgment, the following are reasonable candidates for the Operations Platform:

- **Committee dashboards** — a live view of each committee's goals, initiatives, and health, feeding directly into the Vice President's Chapter 3 review responsibilities.
- **Committee goals tracking** — structured storage for the Mission Framework in Section 5.1, so goals are visible and revisable rather than buried in a document from the start of the semester.
- **Action-item tracking** — the committee-level equivalent of the Executive Board's action-item system from Chapter 3.
- **Facility request generation** — automated pre-population of recurring information, as described in Section 5.5.
- **Marketing request workflow** — reducing the administrative step between Facility Request and Execution in Section 5.4.
- **Committee reports** — standardized formats feeding the Vice President's Executive Meeting preparation, mirroring the Chapter 3 mechanism for committee reporting.
- **Knowledge repository** — the actual system behind Section 5.4's Knowledge Archive step, so Lessons Learned are searchable at the next planning cycle rather than lost in old meeting notes.
- **Meeting notes** — templated capture for the working sessions in Section 5.6.
- **Committee analytics** — surfacing the Success Metrics in Section 7 automatically rather than requiring manual compilation.
- **Primary/Secondary Committee tracking** — visibility into whether Secondary participation is functioning as intended or drifting into the ambiguity risk named in Section 8.
- **Task assignments** — supporting the "assigned responsibilities, owners, deadlines" standard every meeting is expected to end with.

None of the above should extend into deciding a committee's mission, approving an initiative, or evaluating a Committee Chair's performance — those remain human judgment, consistent with the boundary held throughout this handbook.

## Organizational Design Principle

Permanent work belongs to permanent committees. Temporary work belongs to temporary teams.

The chapter should resist creating permanent organizational structures to solve temporary problems. Likewise, recurring mission-critical responsibilities should not depend upon temporary teams.

This distinction preserves organizational clarity, supports long-term scalability, and allows future Executive Boards to adapt without unnecessary restructuring.

---

### 10. Continuous Improvement

Each semester, committees should improve through:

- **Committee self-assessments**, measured against the Success Metrics in Section 7 — not attendance or event counts.
- **Vice President review**, consistent with the Chapter 3 outcome of healthy committees.
- **Committee Chair reflection**, in the same spirit as the Lessons Learned practice this handbook establishes at every level, from New Member Officers in Chapter 2 through Executive Officers in Chapter 3.
- **Member feedback**, gathered directly from Committee Members, not only inferred from attendance.
- **After Action Reviews**, conducted per Section 6, feeding directly into the next planning cycle.
- **Semester planning**, where each committee's Mission Framework is revisited and updated, not simply carried forward unchanged.
- **Lessons learned**, captured per Section 5.4 and archived where the next Committee Chair can actually find and use them.
- **Transition documentation**, prepared as part of the succession planning named in Section 8, so an incoming Committee Chair inherits a working committee rather than a blank slate.
- **Future recommendations**, submitted by outgoing Committee Chairs as part of their transition, feeding into the broader Executive Board's own Continuous Improvement process from Chapter 3.

Every committee should leave the chapter stronger than it found it — the same standard this handbook holds every other system to, now made specific to the level where the mission actually gets done.

Committees are where this handbook stops being a set of ideas and becomes real work. A member's first real experience of leadership, in almost every case, happens inside a committee — not in an Executive Board meeting they haven't yet earned a seat at. That is by design. Every mentoring relationship this chapter has ever built, every scholarship application it has helped a student complete, every event that actually changed something for the community it serves — all of it traces back to a committee, and to the members who showed up and did the work of turning a mission statement into something real. Protecting that is, in the end, what this entire chapter is for.
