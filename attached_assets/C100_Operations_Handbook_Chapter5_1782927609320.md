# FVSU Collegiate 100 Operations Handbook

## Chapter 5: Communication & Information Flow

*Status: Draft for review. Builds on Chapter 1 (v1.0), Chapter 2 (v1.0), Chapter 3 (v1.1), and Chapter 4 (v1.0).*

---

### 1. Purpose

Communication is not simply the act of sending messages. It is the movement of accurate, timely, and actionable information to the people responsible for acting on it. Poor communication creates operational failure even when every other system in this handbook is working correctly — a well-run committee whose initiative never reaches the Vice President's review has still failed, not because the work was bad, but because the information about it never arrived where it needed to.

This chapter defines the chapter's communication *system* — not its communication tools. Tools change. GroupMe gets replaced, the Operations Platform eventually exists, email habits shift with each new officer generation. The system underneath — who owns what information, which channel a given message belongs in, how something escalates when it isn't resolved — should hold steady regardless of which specific tool is carrying it in a given year.

The objective is simple to state and hard to sustain: the right people receive the right information at the right time through the appropriate channel. More messages do not produce better communication. Better systems do — this chapter is that system.

---

### 2. Guiding Principles

**Communication should be timely, accurate, purposeful, professional, actionable, respectful, and documented when appropriate.**
Each of these is a distinct test, not a single vague standard. A message can be timely and accurate but not actionable — informative without telling anyone what to do with the information. A message can be professional and respectful but not purposeful — polite noise. Communication in this chapter is expected to pass all seven tests, not just the easy ones.

**Information should move intentionally, not randomly.**
Every message sent on behalf of the chapter should have a reason for existing, a reason for going to its specific audience, and a reason for using its specific channel. "I wasn't sure where else to put it" is not a communication strategy.

**Communication exists to support execution. It does not replace it.**
A committee that communicates constantly about an initiative but never executes it has not succeeded by this chapter's standard — see Chapter 4's distinction between activity and impact, applied here to communication specifically.

**Every communication should have an owner.**
Not just every system in this handbook — every recurring communication itself. A report with no named owner, a channel with no one responsible for moderating it, an announcement no one is accountable for following up on — each of these is a gap this chapter is built to close. Section 5.5 and Section 5.6 make this operational.

**Communication should reduce uncertainty, not increase activity.**
A chapter that measures its communication health by message volume has the wrong metric. The right measure is whether people know what they need to know, when they need to know it — see Section 7.

**Communication systems should scale with chapter growth.**
What works for informal coordination among fifteen members breaks down at a hundred and fifty. This chapter's structure — defined channels, defined ownership, defined escalation — is built to hold at either size, even if the specific tools carrying it change.

**Technology should improve communication. It should not replace relationships.**
Consistent with Chapter 3's standing rule that technology supports leadership rather than substituting for it, no tool documented in this chapter should become a substitute for the direct, human conversations — mentoring check-ins, accountability conversations, conflict resolution — that this handbook has repeatedly identified as needing to happen in person, not through a platform.

---

### 3. Scope

**This chapter covers:** the chapter's communication hierarchy and channel structure, how questions and issues get routed to their proper owner, how meetings handle communication before, during, and after they happen, the chapter's recurring reporting system, who owns which category of chapter information, and how the chapter preserves organizational memory over time.

**This chapter does not cover:** the detailed content of any single report beyond its structural purpose (Treasurer financial detail lives in Chapter 9, committee-level reporting mechanics live in Chapter 4), event-specific marketing content (Chapter 7), or the substance of Executive Board decision-making itself, which is governed by Chapter 3's Decision-Making Framework — this chapter documents how the information behind those decisions moves, not how the decisions themselves are made.

### 4. Roles & Responsibilities

**Communication Hierarchy.**

```
                              President
                                 │
        ┌────────────────────┼────────────────────┐
        ▼                        ▼                        ▼
  Executive Board              Directors           Executive Support Officers
(VP, Chief of Staff,      (Membership Director,    (Parliamentarian, Historian,
 Secretary, Treasurer,     Communications &          Bylaws Officer)
 Sergeant-at-Arms)          Marketing Director)
        │
        ▼
  Committee Chairs
        │
        ▼
  Committee Members
        │
        ▼
  General Membership
```

- The **President** establishes organizational direction — the "what and why" that everything below flows from — and is the direct reporting line for Directors and Executive Support Officers alike, per Chapter 3.
- The **Chief of Staff** ensures organizational communication, consistent with the Chapter 3 outcome: healthy communication, member engagement, and organizational accountability.
- The **Vice President** ensures committee communication, as the operational expression of the Chapter 3 outcome: healthy committees.
- **Directors** (Membership Director, Communications & Marketing Director) communicate across their functional area chapter-wide, reporting directly to the President rather than through the Vice President's committee line, per Chapter 3.
- **Executive Support Officers** (Parliamentarian, Historian, Bylaws Officer) provide governance, historical, and procedural communication in support of the Executive Board, without supervising members or committees, per Chapter 3.
- **Committee Chairs** ensure communication within their committee, consistent with their Chapter 4 responsibilities.
- **Members** are responsible for reading, responding to, and acting on information relevant to their responsibilities — communication is not complete once it's sent; it's complete once it's received and acted on.

**Communication Responsibilities by Role.**

- **President** — owns strategic and organization-wide communication: direction-setting messages, external representation, and anything that speaks for the chapter as a whole.
- **Vice President** — owns committee-facing communication: relaying Executive direction to Committee Chairs and surfacing committee status upward.
- **Chief of Staff** — owns the chapter's communication *system*, not any specific channel or tool: recurring reports, reminders, follow-ups, executive coordination, communication cadence, and making sure information actually reaches the people responsible for acting on it, per Chapter 3. If the chapter switched from GroupMe to Slack tomorrow, the Chief of Staff's job wouldn't change — the channels in Section 5.1 are incidental to this outcome, not the outcome itself.
- **Secretary** — owns the chapter's documented record of communication: minutes, official correspondence, and the archive that makes past decisions findable later.
- **Treasurer** — owns financial communication: dues reminders, budget status, and financial reporting to the Executive Board.
- **Sergeant-at-Arms** — owns order and respectful conduct during chapter meetings and events, and is the first point of contact for a conduct concern, per the Escalation Model in Section 5.3.
- **Membership Director** — owns recruitment and engagement communication, per Chapter 2, working alongside the Chief of Staff on member engagement specifically, and reporting directly to the President.
- **Communications & Marketing Director** — owns the chapter's overall communications and marketing strategy: social media, flyers, marketing requests, branding, external communications, and the chapter's public image. Reports directly to the President, per Chapter 3's Directors structure.
- **Parliamentarian** — owns communication on parliamentary procedure and constitutional interpretation, working alongside the Sergeant-at-Arms on meeting conduct, per Chapter 2 and Chapter 3.
- **Historian** — owns the chapter's historical record: photos, archives, and the documentation that becomes this chapter's institutional memory over time, per Section 5.6 and Section 5.7.
- **Bylaws Officer** — owns communication regarding the chapter's governing documents: tracking proposed amendments, maintaining the working record of the Bylaws, and advising the Executive Board when a decision touches governance rather than operations.
- **Committee Chairs** — own communication within their committee and reporting upward to the Vice President, per Chapter 4.
- **Committee Members** — own responding to and acting on communication relevant to their committee role.
- **Advisor** — owns institutional guidance communication and serves as the bridge to the Sponsoring Chapter and University, per Chapter 3.

Each role owns communication appropriate to its responsibilities — not everything. A chapter where every officer feels responsible for every message produces duplication and noise; a chapter where communication ownership is this specific produces clarity. Although communication responsibilities are distributed throughout the organization as documented above, every member shares responsibility for communicating professionally, responding in a timely manner, and ensuring information continues moving rather than stopping with them.

### 5. Operational Workflow

**5.1 — Communication Channels**

Not every message belongs in every channel. Each channel exists for a specific purpose:

| Channel | Purpose |
|---|---|
| General Body Meetings | Chapter-wide announcements and decisions requiring full membership presence, per Chapter 3's monthly cadence. |
| Executive Board Meetings | Executive-level coordination and decisions, per Chapter 3. |
| Committee Meetings | Committee-level working sessions, per Chapter 4. |
| Email | Formal, documented communication — anything that needs a paper trail or an official record. |
| GroupMe (or equivalent group chat) | Fast, informal, time-sensitive logistics — not for anything requiring documentation or long-term accountability. |
| iMessage / Text | Direct, individual, urgent communication — not appropriate for chapter-wide decisions or anything that needs to be findable later. |
| Microsoft Teams / Operations Platform | Structured, documented, searchable communication — the intended long-term home for reports and records as the platform matures. |
| Phone Calls | Urgent, sensitive, or nuanced conversations that shouldn't happen over text. |
| In-Person Communication | The highest-bandwidth channel — reserved for anything emotionally sensitive, any mentoring conversation, or any accountability conversation from Chapter 3's Accountability Framework. |

**5.2 — Communication Routing**

Routing answers a different question than escalation (Section 5.3): routing gets a question or need to its rightful owner. Escalation moves something upward because it wasn't resolved at its rightful owner's level, or because its severity requires higher visibility from the start. Keeping this distinction clear matters — collapsing the two makes every unresolved question look like a crisis, and every real crisis look like routine routing.

```
Member question
    ↓
Committee Chair
    ↓
Vice President (if unresolved)
    ↓
President (if necessary)

Financial question
    ↓
Treasurer
    ↓
President (if approval required)

Facility issue
    ↓
Committee Chair
    ↓
Vice President
    ↓
Chief of Staff (to coordinate a fix or communicate a change)
    ↓
President (if necessary)

Technology issue
    ↓
President (default owner — technology is a chapter resource, not a single officer's responsibility)
    ↓
Technology Chair (only if the President has appointed one)

Committee capacity concern
    ↓
Committee Chair
    ↓
Vice President (per Chapter 4's Capacity Management)
```

Technology is no longer a permanent Executive Board seat. Per Chapter 3, technology responsibilities remain with the President by default; a Technology Chair may be appointed when appropriate, but this chapter does not treat that appointment as a standing fixture of the routing model — the President is the routing destination either way, whether or not a Technology Chair happens to be appointed at a given time. The Committee capacity routing is added here because Chapter 4 defines what a Committee Chair should do when a committee reaches capacity, but didn't specify where that conversation is actually routed — this closes that gap directly.

**5.3 — Escalation Model**

```
Committee Issue
    ↓
Committee Chair
    ↓
Vice President
    ↓
President

Conduct Issue
    ↓
Sergeant-at-Arms
    ↓
Chief of Staff
    ↓
President

Governance Issue
    ↓
Parliamentarian
    ↓
President
    ↓
Advisor (if necessary)

Financial Issue
    ↓
Treasurer
    ↓
President
    ↓
Advisor / Parent Chapter (if required)
```

Every escalation path in this chapter terminates with an external authority — the Advisor or Parent Chapter — available as a final step, never as a first one. This is consistent with Chapter 3's Accountability Framework: escalation is a progression, not a shortcut, and the goal is resolution as close to the original owner as possible.

**5.4 — Meeting Communication**

The same three-part discipline applies across Executive Meetings (Chapter 3), Committee Meetings (Chapter 4), and General Body Meetings:

- **Before:** agenda distributed in advance, with enough notice for attendees to prepare; meeting reminders sent through the appropriate channel from Section 5.1.
- **During:** attendance recorded; decisions documented as they're made, not reconstructed afterward; responsibility assignments made explicit in the room, not implied.
- **After:** minutes distributed; action items logged with owners and deadlines, consistent with Chapter 3's action-item standard; follow-up scheduled, not left open-ended.

This section doesn't redefine what happens in Executive or Committee meetings — Chapters 3 and 4 already do that. It states the communication layer that should be consistent across all three meeting types, regardless of which chapter otherwise governs them.

**5.5 — Reporting System**

Reporting is one of the chapter's major operational systems, not a courtesy officers extend to each other. Every report below exists to feed a specific decision or review process already established elsewhere in this handbook — a report that doesn't support a leadership decision is not earning its place on this list.

| Report | Owner | Audience | Frequency | Supports |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Committee Reports | Committee Chair | Vice President | Regular, per Chapter 4 cadence | Vice President's committee health review (Ch. 3, 4) |
| Vice President Summary | Vice President | Executive Board | Weekly, at Executive Meetings | Executive decision-making (Ch. 3) |
| Chief of Staff Member Engagement Report | Chief of Staff | Executive Board | Regular | Membership engagement tracking (Ch. 2) |
| Treasurer Financial Summary | Treasurer | Executive Board | Monthly | Financial sustainability outcome (Ch. 3) |
| Membership Director Recruitment Report | Membership Director | Executive Board / Chief of Staff | Per intake cycle (Sept/Feb) | Membership lifecycle metrics (Ch. 2) |
| Communications & Marketing Report | Communications & Marketing Director | Chief of Staff | Regular | External communication reach, marketing effectiveness, and brand consistency — distinct from internal engagement |
| President Executive Dashboard | President | Advisor / Sponsoring Chapter / chapter as appropriate | Regular | Top-level rollup of all reports above |
| Advisor Summary | Advisor | Sponsoring Chapter / President | Periodic | Continuity and oversight, per Chapter 3's Advisor outcome |

Each report should contain only what its audience needs to act — a Committee Report padded with detail the Vice President doesn't need is not a more thorough report, it's a less useful one.

**5.6 — Information Ownership**

| Information | Owner | Updates | Access | Approves Changes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Meeting Minutes | Secretary | Secretary | Executive Board + relevant committee | Secretary (formatting), President (substantive corrections) |
| Attendance | Secretary (chapter-wide); Committee Chairs (committee-level) | Respective owner | Vice President, Membership Director | N/A — accurate recording only |
| Financial Records | Treasurer | Treasurer | President (full); Executive Board (summary) | President, per Chapter 3's joint accountability |
| Committee Reports | Committee Chair | Committee Chair | Vice President, Executive Board | Vice President |
| Membership Records | Membership Director | Membership Director, Secretary | Executive Board | Membership Director |
| Photos | Historian, per the chapter's Executive Support Officer structure | Historian | Communications & Marketing Director (for posting), chapter archive | Historian |
| Historical Documents | Historian | Historian | Executive Board, incoming officers | Historian, with Advisor input |
| Constitution | The chapter's voting membership, collectively | Amended per Constitutional process only | All members | Two-thirds vote per Constitution Art. XI |
| Bylaws (working record and proposed amendments) | Bylaws Officer | Bylaws Officer | Executive Board | Full Executive Board, with final amendment authority per the Constitution's amendment process |
| Handbook | Executive Board, collectively | Whoever is updating that cycle, with full-board approval per Chapter 1's Standard Practices | All officers, incoming officers | Full Executive Board |
| Platform Data | President, by default; Technology Chair, where appointed | President, or Technology Chair once appointed | Executive Board | President, or Technology Chair once appointed |
| Institutional Knowledge | The chapter itself — not any individual officer | Continuously, through the practices in Section 5.7 | Every officer, current and future | N/A — this is preserved, not approved |

Note the Historian assignment above: the chapter's Executive Support Officer structure already designates the Historian as responsible for maintaining the chapter's history and taking photos at events. This chapter routes Photos and Historical Documents to that existing role rather than inventing a new one. The Bylaws row is new to this table, added because the Bylaws Officer is now a named Executive Support Officer with a natural, direct connection to information ownership that this chapter's model would otherwise leave unassigned.

**5.7 — Organizational Memory**

This section extends Chapter 1's principle that institutional knowledge belongs to the chapter, not to individual officers, into an actual operating practice.

Organizations — student organizations especially — tend to repeat the same mistakes not because the same people make them twice, but because the knowledge of the first mistake left with the officer who learned it. A Committee Chair solves a recurring scheduling conflict with a workaround, graduates, and two years later a different Committee Chair rediscovers the exact same problem from scratch, because nothing connected their experience to the next Chair's.

This chapter treats organizational memory as something actively maintained, not passively hoped for:

- **Knowledge preservation** happens through the Knowledge Archive step in Chapter 4's committee lifecycle, the transition documentation required in Chapter 3, and the Historian's role in Section 5.6 above — three separate mechanisms, not one, because a single point of failure in institutional memory is exactly the risk this handbook has repeatedly worked to eliminate.
- **Officer transitions** are the highest-risk moment for knowledge loss, per Chapter 3's Leadership Succession section — organizational memory practices should be treated as most urgent in the weeks surrounding a transition, not as a background, anytime task.
- **Lessons learned** — from New Member Officers in Chapter 2, from committees in Chapter 4, from the Executive Board in Chapter 3 — should be written down close to when they happen, not reconstructed from memory at the end of the year when the details have already faded.
- **Templates** (agendas, reports, proposals) preserve *how* something was done, not just *that* it was done — a template is often more useful to a future officer than a narrative account, because it can be used immediately.
- **Archives** should be organized so a future officer can find what they need without having to ask someone who was there, which is the actual test of whether organizational memory is working.
- **Historical continuity** is what all of the above adds up to: a chapter where a new President three years from now can understand not just what to do, but why past Executive Boards did it that way — which is the exact standard Chapter 1 sets for this handbook as a whole.

### 6. Standard Practices

**Confidentiality.**

> **Design Principle: Confidentiality should protect people. It should not prevent accountability.**
> These are not in tension as often as they seem — most information can be handled with both standards intact at once. When they do conflict, protecting the person's dignity comes first, but never as a way to avoid a legitimate accountability process already documented elsewhere in this handbook.

- **Member information** (contact details, academic standing, personal circumstances) is shared only with officers who need it for their role, per the ownership table in Section 5.6.
- **Financial information** is visible to the Executive Board at the summary level and to the President and Treasurer at full detail, consistent with Chapter 3's joint accountability model.
- **Executive discussions** — particularly accountability conversations under Chapter 3's framework — remain within the Executive Board unless a formal action requires broader disclosure.
- **Personnel matters** (an officer underperforming, a member facing disciplinary action) are handled through the specific channels Chapter 3 and this chapter's Escalation Model already define, not discussed informally outside them.
- **Meeting minutes** distinguish between what's appropriate for general distribution and what should remain at the Executive level — the Secretary, as owner, makes this determination consistently, not case by case based on who's asking.
- **Platform permissions**, once the Operations Platform exists, should mirror the access column in Section 5.6's ownership table exactly — access should never be broader than what a role actually needs.
- **Appropriate transparency** is the default. Confidentiality is the exception applied deliberately, not the default assumption applied broadly.

**General communication practices:**
- Channel discipline: use the channel matched to the message's purpose (Section 5.1), not whichever channel is fastest to open.
- Response time: officers respond to communication relevant to their role within a consistent, chapter-defined window — a future Executive Board should set this explicitly rather than leaving it implicit.
- Documentation: anything that will matter later — a decision, a commitment, a change — is documented at the time it happens, not reconstructed afterward.
- Reports (Section 5.5) are submitted on their stated cadence without requiring a reminder — a report that only gets submitted when chased has already failed its purpose.

### 7. Success Metrics

Message volume is not a success metric in this chapter. Instead, evaluate:

- **Response time** — whether officers and members respond within the expected window for their role.
- **Report quality and consistency** — whether the reports in Section 5.5 are actually submitted on cadence and actually used in the decisions they're meant to support.
- **Meeting effectiveness** — whether the before/during/after discipline in Section 5.4 is actually being followed, not just documented.
- **Escalation resolution time** — how long it takes an issue to resolve once it enters the Escalation Model in Section 5.3, and how often it resolves before reaching the President.
- **Information bottleneck frequency** — how often a decision or initiative stalls because information didn't reach the right person, tracked as a direct signal of whether this chapter's system is working.
- **Channel appropriateness** — whether messages are actually landing in the channel matched to their purpose, or whether GroupMe has quietly become the channel for everything regardless of Section 5.1's guidance.
- **Knowledge preservation** — whether Section 5.7's practices are producing archives and templates a future officer can actually use, not just producing files that exist.

### 8. Risks & Common Failure Points

- **Information bottlenecks.** The most direct failure of this entire chapter — information stalls at one person, for any reason, and everything downstream of them slows or stops.
- **Too many communication channels.** More channels do not mean more coverage; they mean more places information can get lost, and more cognitive load on members trying to figure out where to look.
- **People bypassing Committee Chairs.** When members or other officers routinely skip the Committee Chair to go straight to the Vice President or President, it undermines the Chair's authority and the entire Communication Hierarchy in Section 4 — even when the bypass is well-intentioned.
- **Important information buried in GroupMe.** GroupMe's speed is exactly why it's unsuited to anything that needs to be findable later — see Section 5.1's explicit purpose boundary for this channel.
- **Poor documentation.** A decision that isn't documented is a decision that will be disputed, forgotten, or repeated unnecessarily.
- **Rumors.** The predictable result of a communication vacuum — when the official channel is slow or unclear, an unofficial one fills the gap, usually with worse information.
- **Communication overload.** The inverse failure of a bottleneck: so much is sent through every channel that nothing stands out as actually requiring attention.
- **Lack of follow-up.** A message sent without a clear next step, or an action item with no follow-up check, produces the appearance of communication without its actual function.
- **The Reporting System becoming performative.** Worth naming explicitly: a report that's produced and filed but never actually referenced in a real decision has failed Section 5.5's core requirement, even if it was submitted perfectly on time.
- **Routing and Escalation getting blurred together.** If the distinction in Section 5.2 and 5.3 isn't maintained in practice, every unresolved question starts looking like a crisis, and the Escalation Model loses its ability to signal genuine severity.

### 9. Future Platform Opportunities

Consistent with this handbook's standing rule, automation here should reduce administrative work while preserving human leadership — never substituting for the relationships this chapter's principles depend on:

- **Automated reminders** — for reports (Section 5.5), meeting prep (Section 5.4), and action-item follow-up.
- **Officer dashboards** — a per-role view of what communication is owed and to whom, based on Section 4's ownership model.
- **Executive reports and committee reports** — templated generation, reducing the administrative load of Section 5.5 without touching what gets included.
- **Notification routing** — automatically directing a message to the right owner per Section 5.2's routing model.
- **Approval workflows** — supporting the approval gates already defined in Chapters 3 and 4, not creating new ones.
- **Document storage and knowledge repository** — the actual system behind Section 5.7's organizational memory practices.
- **Meeting archives** — a searchable record supporting Section 5.4's documentation requirement.
- **Calendar integration** — tying the Executive Calendar (Chapter 3) and committee planning (Chapter 4) into a single view.
- **Task management** — supporting action-item tracking across every chapter this handbook has established so far.
- **Role-based notifications** — ensuring information reaches people based on their actual role, mirroring Section 5.6's access model.
- **Communication analytics** — surfacing the Success Metrics in Section 7 automatically.

None of the above should make a judgment call about what to communicate or how to resolve an escalation — those remain human decisions, consistent with the boundary held throughout this handbook.

### 10. Continuous Improvement

Each semester, the chapter's communication system should be evaluated through:

- **Officer feedback** on whether the ownership model in Section 4 and 5.6 is actually working for them.
- **Member feedback** on whether information is reaching them in a usable way — the people receiving communication are often the first to notice when a system has quietly broken down.
- **Communication audits** — a periodic review of whether messages are actually landing in the channels defined in Section 5.1.
- **Channel evaluation** — whether a channel's actual use still matches its documented purpose, or has drifted.
- **Response time review**, measured against Section 7's standard.
- **Report quality review**, checking whether Section 5.5's reports are still supporting real decisions.
- **Meeting effectiveness review**, per Section 5.4.
- **Knowledge preservation review**, per Section 5.7.
- **Technology improvements**, evaluated against Section 9's boundary — every new tool considered should be tested against whether it improves communication or merely adds another channel to Section 8's risk list.

A chapter's communication system, more than almost any other in this handbook, will drift quietly if it isn't actively maintained — channels multiply, ownership blurs, and information that used to move cleanly starts catching on people who were never meant to be a bottleneck. The standard here is the same one this handbook holds everywhere else: each Executive Board should leave this system clearer than they found it, not just busier.
