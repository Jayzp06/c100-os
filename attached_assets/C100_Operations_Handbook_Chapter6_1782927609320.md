# FVSU Collegiate 100 Operations Handbook

## Chapter 6: Event & Project Operations

*Status: Draft for review. Builds on Chapter 1 (v1.0), Chapter 2 (v1.0), Chapter 3 (v1.1), Chapter 4 (v1.0), and Chapter 5 (v1.0).*

---

### 1. Purpose

Events are not the purpose of this organization. Events are one method by which the organization fulfills its mission — the same status Chapter 4 already gives them at the committee level. This chapter exists to define a single, repeatable operational system for planning, approving, executing, evaluating, and preserving the knowledge from every organizational initiative — not a guide to hosting events specifically.

The same workflow documented here should work equally well for an educational workshop, a mentoring activity, a community service project, a professional development program, a recruitment initiative, a fundraiser, a conference, an induction ceremony, or a Special Events Team project. If a step in this chapter only makes sense for one type of initiative, it doesn't belong in the core lifecycle — it belongs in a type-specific note within the Event Planning Framework in Section 5.3.

Every initiative documented under this system should advance the mission, be properly planned, receive appropriate review, be adequately resourced, be effectively communicated, be successfully executed, be evaluated afterward, and improve the initiatives that follow it. This chapter exists to make that happen with the least administrative burden that still preserves real accountability — not the least burden possible.

---

### 2. Guiding Principles

**Mission before events. Planning before execution.**
An initiative that exists because it sounded fun, rather than because it advances a specific outcome in a Committee or Team's mission, has skipped the first question this chapter asks. Planning exists to make execution boring in the best sense — no surprises, because the surprises were already thought through in advance.

**Preparation prevents emergencies.**
Most of what shows up in Section 5.8's Risk Management as a crisis was, in fact, predictable. The purpose of planning is not to eliminate every possible problem — it's to make sure the problems that do occur are the ones nobody could have reasonably anticipated, not the ones the planning process should have caught.

**Every initiative has an owner.**
This is the same standard Chapter 5 sets for communication, applied here to execution: an initiative with no single accountable Project Owner is not a valid initiative under this chapter, regardless of how good the idea is. Section 5.2 makes this operational.

> **Design Principle: Administrative work should be minimized. Accountability should not.**
> This is the same principle Chapter 4 elevated for committee operations, and it governs this chapter with equal weight. Reducing paperwork and pre-populating forms are worth pursuing everywhere this chapter touches the Operations Platform (Section 9) — but never as a way to make an initiative less accountable, only less burdensome to run well.

**Every initiative should leave behind knowledge for the next one.**
An initiative that ends at Execution, without reaching Knowledge Archive, has only produced an event — not the organizational learning this chapter exists to accumulate. See Section 5.9.

**Execution quality matters more than event quantity.**
A chapter that runs fewer, better-executed initiatives has succeeded by this chapter's standard more than one that runs many mediocre ones — see Section 7 for how this is actually measured, not just stated.

**Successful projects build stronger committees. Successful committees build stronger chapters.**
This chapter's ultimate justification is not the events themselves — it's what running them well does for the people and committees behind them. An initiative should leave its Project Owner, its volunteers, and its sponsoring Committee or Team more capable than before, consistent with the leadership development standard running through every chapter of this handbook.

### 3. Scope

**This chapter covers:** the complete lifecycle any chapter initiative moves through, from the moment a need is identified to the moment its knowledge is archived for future use — regardless of whether that initiative originates in a Standing Committee, an Operational Support Team, or the Executive Board itself.

**This chapter does not cover:** the detailed financial policy governing budgets, purchases, and reimbursements (Chapter 9 — this chapter references the Treasurer's role and defers the policy itself), the internal mission-setting and governance of Standing Committees themselves (Chapter 4 — this chapter picks up once a committee has an idea it wants to execute), or the mechanics of the chapter's communication channels (Chapter 5 — this chapter uses those channels without redefining them). This chapter builds on all three; it does not replace any of them.

---

### 4. Roles & Responsibilities

**Project Ownership.**

Every initiative has exactly one **Project Owner**, assigned at the Project Owner Assigned step in Section 5.1. The Project Owner may be:
- A Committee Chair
- An Operational Support Team Lead (per Chapter 4)
- An Executive Officer
- The President or Vice President
- Another appointed coordinator

Regardless of who holds it, the Project Owner is responsible for planning, coordination, communication, delegation, execution, reporting, the After Action Review, and knowledge preservation for that specific initiative. **The Project Owner owns outcomes. Delegation does not remove accountability** — a Project Owner who hands off setup to a volunteer team is still accountable if setup fails, in the same way a Committee Chair remains accountable for a committee's outcome even when work is distributed among members.

**Supporting roles, per initiative:**

- **Vice President** — reviews initiative readiness before it proceeds past planning, consistent with the Chapter 3 outcome of healthy committees, extended here to cover Operational Support Teams as well.
- **Treasurer** — reviews financial implications where a budget is involved; see Section 5.5 for the boundary between this chapter and Chapter 9.
- **Communications & Marketing Director** — owns the marketing workflow in Section 5.6, in coordination with the Project Owner.
- **Chief of Staff** — coordinates communication and organizational timelines around the initiative, consistent with the communication-system ownership established in Chapter 5.
- **President** — approves major initiatives where appropriate, consistent with Chapter 4's precedent.
- **Sergeant-at-Arms** — owns order, professionalism, and conduct during the initiative itself, consistent with the Chapter 3 outcome, most directly exercised during Day-of Operations (Section 5.7).
- **Historian** — documents the initiative through photos and archival records, consistent with the Information Ownership model in Chapter 5.
- **Advisor** — receives visibility into the initiative before execution, per the Advisor Awareness step in Section 5.1, consistent with the gap Chapter 4 closed and this chapter preserves.

### 5. Operational Workflow

**5.1 — Event & Project Lifecycle**

```
Mission Need Identified
        ↓
Initiative Classification
        ↓
   ┌────────────────────┬─────────────────────────────┬───────────────────────┐
   ▼                                    ▼                                     ▼
Committee Initiative      Operational Support Team Initiative        Executive Initiative
   └────────────────────┴─────────────────────────────┴───────────────────────┘
        ↓
Discussion  (Committee, Team, or Executive Board —
             wherever the idea originates)
        ↓
Project Proposal Developed
        ↓
Sponsoring Body Review  (Committee Chair, Team Lead, or
                          Executive Officer confirms it's ready to advance)
        ↓
Project Owner Assigned
        ↓
Vice President Review  ─────┐
        ↓                    │  visibility, not approval —
Executive Visibility          │  see note below
        ↓                    │
Advisor Awareness  ──────────┘
        ↓
Budget Review (if required)
        ↓
Facility Request (if required, also satisfies University submission)
        ↓
Marketing Request
        ↓
Logistics Planning
        ↓
Risk Review
        ↓
Execution
        ↓
Immediate Debrief
        ↓
After Action Review
        ↓
Lessons Learned
        ↓
Knowledge Archive  ─────▶ feeds back into the originating Committee or Team's
                           mission framework, and this chapter's template library
```

**Initiative Classification** is added as its own step, immediately after Mission Need Identified, because every step from Sponsoring Body Review onward depends on knowing which kind of initiative this is:

- **Committee Initiative** — originates within a Standing Committee's ongoing mission (Chapter 4). Sponsoring Body Review is conducted by the Committee Chair, and the Project Owner is typically the Chair or a Project Lead delegated within the committee.
- **Operational Support Team Initiative** — originates from a temporary, objective-specific need addressed by an Operational Support Team (Chapter 4). Sponsoring Body Review is conducted by the Team Lead, and the initiative exists only for the duration of that team's assignment, consistent with Chapter 4's Organizational Design Principle that temporary work belongs to temporary teams.
- **Executive Initiative** — originates directly from the Executive Board or President rather than from a specific Committee or Team, typically because it's strategic in nature or doesn't naturally belong to a single committee's mission. Sponsoring Body Review is conducted by the relevant Executive Officer.

Classifying the initiative this early doesn't change any step later in the lifecycle — every classification moves through the same sequence from Project Owner Assigned onward. What changes is only who conducts the Sponsoring Body Review and who the default pool of Project Owner candidates is. This keeps the lifecycle genuinely uniform across initiative types, consistent with Section 1's requirement that the same system work for every kind of initiative, while still making explicit what was previously only implied by the parenthetical in the Discussion and Sponsoring Body Review steps.

Four other changes from the suggested starting framework are worth explaining, since this chapter was asked to challenge and improve it rather than simply transcribe it:

**"Committee Chair Review" is generalized to "Sponsoring Body Review."** This chapter is explicitly meant to scale across Standing Committees, Operational Support Teams, and Executive-sourced initiatives (Section 1). A step that only names the Committee Chair would leave Team Leads and Executive Officers without an equivalent checkpoint. The function is identical regardless of source — whoever originated the idea confirms it's actually ready before a Project Owner is formally assigned.

**Advisor Awareness is restored, not dropped.** Chapter 4 deliberately added this step to close a real gap — the Advisor has no visibility into planning otherwise, despite the National Policy Manual expecting exactly that visibility. A general-purpose lifecycle that quietly drops this step would reintroduce the gap Chapter 4 closed, and would do so at the level — Operational Support Teams and Executive-sourced initiatives — where the Advisor is least likely to already be in the loop informally.

**Logistics Planning and Risk Review are kept as distinct steps**, not folded into Facility Request or Marketing Request. Logistics (Section 5.3's framework: transportation, materials, food, volunteers) and Risk (Section 5.8) are genuinely different bodies of work from facility booking and marketing — collapsing them would be simplifying the workflow to reduce steps, which this chapter was specifically told not to do.

**Immediate Debrief is added as its own step, distinct from the After Action Review.** These serve different purposes and should not be merged. An Immediate Debrief happens the same day or the next, while impressions are fresh but before they've been processed — a quick capture of what stood out, positive or negative. The After Action Review (Section 5.9) happens with more distance, using the Immediate Debrief as raw material alongside attendance data, budget actuals, and volunteer feedback. Merging them risks losing the immediate, unfiltered reaction in favor of only the more considered, structured one.

**5.2 — Project Ownership**

Section 4 establishes who can serve as Project Owner. This section defines what the role actually does across the lifecycle in Section 5.1:

- **Planning** — developing the proposal and the Event Planning Framework (Section 5.3) for the specific initiative.
- **Coordination** — keeping every supporting role in Section 4 aligned and moving on the same timeline.
- **Communication** — consistent with Chapter 5, ensuring the right people have the right information at each lifecycle stage, not just at kickoff.
- **Delegation** — assigning specific pieces of the work to volunteers or committee members without losing accountability for the outcome.
- **Execution** — present and responsible during Section 5.7's Day-of Operations, even when specific tasks are delegated.
- **Reporting** — status updates through the lifecycle, and a final report feeding into Section 5.9.
- **After Action Review** — leading the structured reflection in Section 5.9, not simply attending it.
- **Knowledge preservation** — ensuring the initiative actually reaches Knowledge Archive, not just Execution.

**5.3 — Event Planning Framework**

This framework should become the chapter standard for every initiative, regardless of type. Not every field applies to every initiative — a small mentoring session doesn't need the same depth as a conference — but every initiative should at least consider each field rather than skip it by default.

| Field | Captures |
|---|---|
| Purpose | Why this initiative exists. |
| Mission Alignment | Which committee or chapter mission outcome this advances. |
| Target Audience | Who this initiative is actually for. |
| Desired Outcomes | What success looks like, stated before execution, not after. |
| Date / Time / Location | The basic logistics footprint. |
| Budget | Estimated cost, per Section 5.5. |
| Partners | Any external individuals or organizations involved. |
| Committee Responsibilities | What the sponsoring Committee or Team specifically owns. |
| Marketing | Per Section 5.6. |
| Materials | What needs to be sourced, printed, or prepared. |
| Transportation | If applicable. |
| Volunteers | Per Section 5.7. |
| Food | If applicable. |
| Risk Considerations | Per Section 5.8. |
| Accessibility | Whether the initiative is reachable and usable by everyone it's meant to serve. |
| Cleanup | Who owns it and when it happens. |
| Documentation | What's being captured for Section 5.9's Knowledge Preservation. |
| Follow-Up | What happens after execution — a thank-you, a survey, a next step. |
| Evaluation | How this specific initiative will be judged, tied to Desired Outcomes above. |

*Note on existing planning materials:* where previously developed planning materials — for example, existing Mentoring program planning documents — are available, they should be treated as worked examples of this framework's philosophy, not templates to copy without adaptation. This chapter's framework is the standard; prior materials are useful references for how that standard has been applied in practice, and should be reviewed against this table rather than assumed to already match it.

**5.4 — Facility Reservation Process**

This expands the workflow Chapter 4 introduced, with the added detail a full event lifecycle requires:

1. Planning timeline works backward from University submission deadlines — these are a hard external constraint, not a chapter preference, per Chapter 4.
2. The Project Owner completes a standardized facility request once Budget Review has cleared, if a budget is involved.
3. Required approvals follow Chapter 4's model: Vice President reviews readiness, Treasurer reviews financial implications where relevant, President approves major initiatives.
4. Room setup requests, equipment requests, food requests, and vendor requests are documented as part of the same request rather than as afterthoughts discovered during Logistics Planning (Section 5.1).
5. External partner coordination — anyone outside the chapter involved in the space or its use — is confirmed before the request is finalized, not during Execution.

The Operations Platform should eventually pre-populate recurring information (Advisor, chapter information, contact information, per Chapter 4) so the Project Owner only completes what's specific to this initiative — while preserving every approval step above. Pre-population reduces administrative work; it does not reduce who has to approve what.

**5.5 — Budget Integration**

This section references the Treasurer's responsibilities without redefining them — the detailed financial policy governing all of the items below lives in Chapter 9: Financial Operations.

At the initiative level, the Project Owner is responsible for: submitting a budget request with estimated costs as part of the Event Planning Framework, securing funding approval before committing chapter funds, tracking purchases against the approved budget, submitting reimbursements per Chapter 9's process, and coordinating with any fundraiser tied to the initiative. Financial accountability for the initiative rests with the Project Owner day to day, with the Treasurer and President holding the joint accountability Chapter 3 establishes for anything above the routine threshold.

**5.6 — Marketing Workflow**

Marketing exists to support mission execution — not simply to advertise an event. A well-marketed initiative that doesn't actually advance its stated mission outcome (Section 5.3) has succeeded at marketing and failed at this chapter's actual standard.

The relationship between roles:
- The **Project Owner** initiates the marketing request as part of the lifecycle in Section 5.1, providing the initiative's purpose, audience, and key details.
- The **Committee Chair** (where the Project Owner is not the Chair) confirms the request reflects the committee's mission accurately.
- The **Communications & Marketing Director** owns execution: flyer creation, social media content, announcements, email communication, and the overall promotion timeline, consistent with the full scope of that role established in Chapter 5.
- The **Chief of Staff** ensures the promotion timeline is coordinated with the chapter's broader communication cadence, so one initiative's marketing doesn't drown out another's.
- The **President** is aware of any marketing that speaks on behalf of the chapter's public image at a strategic level, consistent with the President's ownership of organization-wide communication in Chapter 5.

Photography during the initiative connects to the Historian's role in Chapter 5's Information Ownership model — the Communications & Marketing Director uses photos for active promotion, but the Historian owns the archival record. Post-event communication (thank-yous, recap content, impact summaries) closes the loop and should be planned during Logistics Planning, not improvised afterward.

**5.7 — Volunteer Coordination & Day-of Operations**

*Volunteer Coordination* covers: recruitment, assignments, training, checklists, day-of responsibilities, backup staffing, recognition, and ongoing volunteer communication. Every volunteer should know their specific assignment before the day of the initiative — assigning roles the morning of is a planning failure, not a day-of adjustment.

*Day-of Operations* is a standardized execution checklist, consistent across every initiative type this chapter covers:
- Arrival and setup
- Equipment check
- Registration
- Attendance tracking
- Speaker or facilitator coordination
- Volunteer management, including backup staffing if someone doesn't show
- Photography (Historian, per Section 5.6)
- Issue resolution — a named point of contact for problems as they arise, not a scramble to figure out who's in charge
- Cleanup
- Closing responsibilities, including confirming the space is returned to its required condition

The **Sergeant-at-Arms**, consistent with the Chapter 3 outcome of professionalism, order, and integrity during official chapter functions, has a natural role during Day-of Operations specifically — not just at formal chapter meetings. Every initiative under this chapter counts as an official chapter function for that purpose.

**5.8 — Risk Management**

Risk Management here is distinct from Section 8's Risks & Common Failure Points: this section covers risks to a specific initiative's execution; Section 8 covers risks to this chapter's system as a whole. Common operational risks include: weather, low attendance, speaker cancellation, facility issues, technology failure, transportation issues, supply shortages, budget overruns, volunteer shortages, the need for emergency communication, and last-minute schedule changes.

Two improvements to how this list should actually be used, rather than just referenced:

**Every identified risk needs a named mitigation owner, not just a listed possibility.** A risk list that says "weather" without saying who checks the forecast and who makes the call to adjust is not a completed Risk Review — it's an acknowledgment that risk exists, which isn't the same thing.

**Risk Review should scale to the initiative's size, consistent with Chapter 4's Capacity Management principle.** A small mentoring session and a full conference should not receive the same depth of Risk Review — treating them identically either overburdens the small initiative or underprepares the large one. The Project Owner, with Vice President input, should scope the Risk Review to match the initiative's actual complexity and stakes.

**5.9 — After Action Review & Knowledge Preservation**

This is intended to be one of the strongest sections in this chapter, and it connects directly to Chapter 5's Organizational Memory section — everything below is that section's practices, applied at the level of a single initiative.

Every initiative concludes with a structured After Action Review, guided by:
- Did we accomplish our mission?
- Did we achieve the intended outcomes stated in Section 5.3?
- What exceeded expectations?
- What failed?
- What surprised us?
- What should never happen again?
- What should become standard practice?
- What templates should be updated?
- What should future officers know?

Every initiative should leave behind: planning documents, the budget, marketing materials, photos, attendance records, lessons learned, vendor information, facility contacts, templates, checklists, and recommendations for the next Project Owner. Nothing should depend solely on someone's memory — this is the same standard Chapter 1 sets for the whole chapter, made concrete at the level where it's most often lost: the individual initiative, run once, remembered by whoever happened to run it, unless this section's practice is actually followed.

### 6. Standard Practices

- Planning timelines are set working backward from the initiative's date, through Facility Request deadlines (Section 5.4), to the earliest point Discussion should begin — a Project Owner should know their full timeline before committing to a date.
- Documentation happens as the initiative progresses, not reconstructed afterward — this is the initiative-level instance of Chapter 5's standing documentation practice.
- Communication around the initiative follows Chapter 5's channel guidance exactly — no initiative gets its own separate communication rules.
- Review cycles: Vice President Review and Executive Visibility (Section 5.1) happen on a predictable cadence tied to the Executive Meeting rhythm from Chapter 3, not on an ad hoc basis per initiative.
- The Event Planning Framework (Section 5.3) is completed before Budget Review, not filled in retroactively to justify a request already made informally.

### 7. Success Metrics

Execution quality matters more than event quantity — this chapter's success metrics reflect that directly:

- **Mission alignment** — whether initiatives actually advance the outcome stated in their Event Planning Framework, not just whether they happened.
- **Planning completeness** — whether the Event Planning Framework in Section 5.3 was actually completed, not skipped under time pressure.
- **Execution quality** — whether Day-of Operations (Section 5.7) ran according to plan, and how well deviations were handled when they occurred.
- **Knowledge preservation rate** — the percentage of initiatives that actually reach Knowledge Archive, not just Execution.
- **After Action Review completion** — whether Section 5.9's structured reflection actually happens, including for initiatives that went well, not only the ones that struggled.
- **Budget accuracy** — how closely actual costs tracked the estimate in Section 5.3, feeding into better estimates for future initiatives.
- **Volunteer experience** — whether volunteers were clearly briefed, adequately supported, and would volunteer again.
- **Repeat-initiative ease** — whether a similar future initiative is measurably easier to plan because of the template and lessons this one left behind, which is the most direct test of whether Section 5.9 is actually working.

### 8. Risks & Common Failure Points

These are systemic risks to this chapter's operation as a whole — distinct from Section 5.8's per-initiative risk checklist:

- **Project Owner ambiguity.** When it isn't clear whether the Project Owner is the Committee Chair, a Team Lead, or someone else entirely, accountability quietly diffuses across everyone and no one — the exact failure Section 4 and 5.2 exist to prevent.
- **Project Owner burnout.** A Project Owner who personally handles every responsibility in Section 5.2 rather than delegating within it is on the same path Chapter 3 and Chapter 4 warn against for Executive Officers and Committee Chairs — delegation without losing accountability is the standard, not personally doing everything.
- **After Action Review skipped for "successful" events.** The single most common failure mode of this entire chapter: a well-run initiative feels like it doesn't need reflection, so Section 5.9 gets skipped, and the specific reasons it went well are never captured for the next Project Owner to repeat deliberately.
- **Marketing bypassing the workflow.** A flyer or post that goes out without moving through Section 5.6's relationship model undermines both the Communications & Marketing Director's ownership and the Chief of Staff's coordination of the chapter's overall communication cadence.
- **Facility deadlines missed due to late planning.** University submission deadlines are fixed; a Project Owner who starts planning too late doesn't get to negotiate that constraint, which is why Standard Practices in Section 6 requires timelines to work backward from the date, not forward from when planning happens to start.
- **Shadow initiatives.** Events or projects that happen without ever formally entering the lifecycle in Section 5.1 — run informally by a committee or member without a named Project Owner, without Executive Visibility, and without Advisor Awareness. These carry real risk (Section 5.8) with none of this chapter's safeguards, and are a direct violation of "every initiative has an owner."
- **Knowledge Archive treated as optional under time pressure.** The step most likely to be skipped when a Project Owner is tired at the end of an initiative — and the step whose absence costs the chapter the most later, since it's the only one of these failure modes that damages every future initiative rather than just this one.

### 9. Future Platform Opportunities

Consistent with this handbook's standing rule, automation here should eliminate repetitive administration — not leadership:

- **Project dashboards** — a live view of every active initiative's lifecycle stage, feeding Vice President and Executive Visibility reviews.
- **Timeline tracking** — automated backward-planning from an initiative's date through Section 5.4's deadlines.
- **Facility request generation** — pre-population per Section 5.4, preserving every approval step.
- **Marketing request workflow** — routing per Section 5.6's relationship model.
- **Volunteer management** — recruitment, assignment, and communication support for Section 5.7.
- **Approval workflows** — supporting the gates already defined in Section 5.1, not creating new ones.
- **Budget requests** — structured intake feeding Section 5.5 and Chapter 9.
- **Attendance tracking** — supporting Section 5.7's Day-of checklist and Section 7's metrics.
- **Task management** — delegation tracking for the Project Owner's responsibilities in Section 5.2.
- **Risk checklist** — structured capture for Section 5.8, including named mitigation owners.
- **After Action Review templates** — structured capture for Section 5.9's questions.
- **Knowledge archive and template library** — the actual system behind Section 5.9's knowledge preservation standard.
- **Recurring event cloning** — for initiatives that repeat (an annual induction ceremony, a recurring workshop), reducing the administrative work of re-entering a Framework that's already mostly known.

None of the above should extend into deciding whether an initiative should happen, approving a budget, or evaluating an After Action Review's substance — those remain human judgment, consistent with the boundary held throughout this handbook.

---

### 10. Continuous Improvement

Each semester, this system should improve through:

- **Project reviews** — a rollup of the After Action Reviews (Section 5.9) completed that semester, looking for patterns across initiatives, not just within them.
- **Committee and Team feedback** — whether the lifecycle in Section 5.1 is actually working for the people running initiatives through it, or creating friction the chapter should address.
- **Member feedback** — from volunteers and attendees, not only from officers.
- **Executive review**, consistent with Chapter 3's Continuous Improvement process.
- **Template improvements** — updating the Event Planning Framework and AAR template based on what actually got used and what got skipped.
- **Workflow improvements** — revisiting the lifecycle itself if a step consistently gets bypassed, since a step nobody follows is a signal the workflow needs adjustment, not just stricter enforcement.
- **Knowledge preservation review** — whether the Knowledge Archive is actually being used by new Project Owners, or just accumulating unread.
- **Platform enhancements**, evaluated against Section 9's boundary.

Every completed initiative should make the next one easier, and every initiative should strengthen the organization beyond the event itself — attendance and applause are not the standard; a stronger committee, a better-prepared volunteer, and a template the next Project Owner doesn't have to build from scratch are.
