# FVSU Collegiate 100 Operations Handbook

## Chapter 7: Financial Operations

*Status: Draft for review — Version 1.0 Candidate. Builds on Chapters 1–6 (all approved).*

*A note on how this chapter is built, different from the chapters before it: where chapter leadership has not yet decided a specific financial policy, this chapter does not invent one. Instead, it identifies the decision, explains why it matters, recommends a scalable framework to decide within, and marks it clearly as a* **Leadership Decision Required***. This chapter is an operations manual for the financial system that already exists, and a roadmap for the policy decisions that don't exist yet — it is not a substitute for chapter leadership actually making those decisions.*

---

### 1. Purpose

Money is not the mission. Money enables the mission. Every financial process documented in this chapter should ultimately trace back to supporting service, leadership development, mentorship, and the organizational sustainability Chapter 1 establishes as a first principle.

Financial operations exist to create trust — with members who pay dues, with the Sponsoring Chapter that holds the chapter accountable, with the University that recognizes it, and with the youth and community the chapter's programming ultimately serves. Trust comes from transparency. Transparency comes from documented process, applied consistently, not from any individual officer's personal integrity alone — however trustworthy that officer may be. This chapter documents the process that makes trust structural rather than personal.

---

### 2. Guiding Principles

**Mission before money. Planning before spending.**
A budget exists to serve an initiative's mission outcome (Chapter 6), not the reverse — spending should never happen simply because funds are available.

**No financial process should rely entirely on one individual.**
This is the organizing idea behind Section 5.5's Separation of Duties, and it governs every other principle in this chapter. A financial system with a single point of failure is a risk to the chapter regardless of how capable that individual is.

**Financial transparency builds organizational trust.**
Consistent with Chapter 5's confidentiality standard — protecting people, not preventing accountability — financial transparency here means the right people can always see what they need to see, not that every financial detail is broadcast to everyone.

> **Design Principle: Administrative work should be minimized. Financial accountability should not.**
> The same principle Chapter 4 and Chapter 6 elevate for their respective systems, holding with equal weight here. Every future Operations Platform feature evaluated against this chapter (Section 9) should reduce the effort of documenting a transaction — never the number of people who have to know about it or approve it.

**Budgets are planning tools, not spending targets.**
An allocated budget is a plan for what a committee or initiative expects to need — it is not, by itself, authorization to spend up to that amount. Section 5.2 makes this distinction operational.

**Financial systems should survive officer transitions.**
A Treasurer's personal spreadsheet, password, or informal process is not a financial system under this chapter's standard — it's a personal habit that happens to be currently useful. Section 5.6 and Section 10 exist to make sure the actual system survives the person.

---

### 3. Scope

**This chapter covers:** annual and semester budgets, committee and project budgets, fundraisers, conference expenses, member and National dues, cash and digital payment handling, donations, sponsorships, merchandise, expense approvals, reimbursements, financial reporting and records, and the financial dimension of officer transitions.

**This chapter does not cover:** the specific dollar thresholds, custody arrangements, and procedural details that chapter leadership has not yet decided — those are marked as Leadership Decisions Required throughout, not fabricated here. It also does not cover the operational mechanics of how an initiative's budget need first arises (Chapter 6 — this chapter picks up once a budget request enters the financial system) or the chapter's general communication channels (Chapter 5 — this chapter uses those channels for financial reporting without redefining them).

### 4. Roles & Responsibilities

**Current State vs. Future State.**

These are documented separately because they involve a real structural difference, not just a stylistic one:

- **Current State:** the Parent Chapter (100 Black Men of Macon-Middle Georgia Area, Inc.) maintains custody of chapter funds. The Collegiate Chapter manages planning, requests, budgeting, and internal financial tracking, but financial transactions requiring chapter funds are coordinated through the Advisor and Parent Chapter before funds actually move.
- **Future State:** the Collegiate Chapter may eventually maintain its own financial account. This chapter's framework is built so that transition — if and when chapter leadership decides to pursue it — does not require rewriting this chapter, only updating which role holds Custody in the table below.

> **Leadership Decision Required: Independent Chapter Banking.**
> Whether and when the chapter pursues its own financial account, separate from the Parent Chapter's custody, is a decision for chapter leadership — likely in coordination with the Advisor and Parent Chapter, given the oversight relationship established in Chapter 3. This chapter does not recommend a timeline for that decision; it only ensures the operational framework already accommodates it when made.

**Financial Roles.**

No one holds financial authority simply by holding an office. This chapter differentiates four distinct responsibilities — **Authorization**, **Custody**, **Recordkeeping**, and **Oversight** — and assigns them separately wherever practical, rather than defaulting all four to the Treasurer by habit:

| Role | Authorization | Custody | Recordkeeping | Oversight |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| President | Major/strategic spending, per Chapter 3's decision-making thresholds | No | No | Overall financial health, per the Chapter 3 outcome |
| Treasurer | Routine spending within an approved budget allocation | **Current State: No** (Parent Chapter holds custody) — **Future State: Yes**, if an independent account is established | Yes — the chapter's financial system of record | No (Recordkeeping and Oversight are intentionally separated — see Section 5.5) |
| Vice President | No | No | No | Committee-level budget requests, before they reach Treasurer Review |
| Chief of Staff | No | No | No | Ensures financial deadlines and reports are communicated on the cadence established in Chapter 5 |
| Committee Chairs / Project Owners | Initiating a budget request (Chapter 6), not final authorization | No | Committee/initiative-level expense tracking, feeding the Treasurer's record | No |
| Advisor | Coordinates release of Parent-Chapter-held funds in the Current State | No | No | Continuity check across officer transitions, per the Chapter 3 Advisor outcome |
| Parent Chapter | Final authorization for anything requiring release of custody-held funds, Current State | **Yes, Current State** | No | Compliance with National financial policy |

This table should be treated as a living reference — if the chapter moves to the Future State, only the Custody column and the roles it touches need to change, not the rest of this chapter.

### 5. Operational Workflow

**5.1 — Financial Workflow**

```
Financial Need Identified  (often arising from a Chapter 6 initiative's
                             Budget field in the Event Planning Framework)
        ↓
Budget Request
        ↓
Treasurer Review
        ↓
President Review
        ↓
Advisor / Parent Chapter Coordination  (Current State — see Section 4)
        ↓
Funding Authorization
        ↓
Purchase
        ↓
Receipt Collection
        ↓
Expense Documentation
        ↓
Financial Report
        ↓
Record Archive
```

One deliberate change from the suggested starting framework: **Budget Review and Continuous Improvement are not steps in this per-transaction lifecycle.** The suggested version placed them at the end of every single financial request, but they operate on a different timescale entirely — Budget Review happens periodically, evaluating the aggregate of everything that reached Record Archive across a semester, and Continuous Improvement (Section 10) operates annually. Folding a semester-level review into every individual purchase would mean either skipping it constantly (since no one reviews a whole semester's budget after buying supplies for one meeting) or making minor purchases carry disproportionate process weight. This chapter separates the transaction lifecycle above from the periodic review it feeds, the same way Chapter 6 separated a single initiative's Immediate Debrief from the chapter-wide Continuous Improvement process.

This workflow scales to the size of the request, consistent with the proportionality principle established in Chapter 6's Risk Review — a small, pre-approved committee purchase within an existing budget allocation moves through this lifecycle quickly; a large or unusual request receives more scrutiny at Treasurer and President Review. The steps don't change; the depth of review at each step does.

> **Leadership Decision Required: Spending Approval Thresholds.**
> This handbook does not set a specific dollar amount separating "routine, pre-approved" spending from spending that requires escalated review. Until chapter leadership sets one, every expenditure should default to requiring both Treasurer and President Review, per Section 4's model — treat this as the conservative default, not a permanent policy.

> **Leadership Decision Required: Emergency Expenditures.**
> This handbook does not define what qualifies as a financial emergency or who may authorize spending outside the normal workflow above when one occurs. Until decided, no expenditure should bypass Treasurer and President Review, even under time pressure — an emergency justifies moving quickly through the steps, not skipping them.

**5.2 — Committee Budget Model**

Committee budgeting should encourage planning — a committee that thinks through its semester's financial needs in advance, consistent with Chapter 4's Semester Planning rhythm, is better positioned than one that requests funds reactively, initiative by initiative.

Three distinct concepts should never be treated as interchangeable:

- **Budget Allocation** — the amount planned for a committee or initiative over a period. This is a planning figure, not a spending authorization.
- **Expense Approval** — the specific authorization for an individual purchase. An expense within an existing allocation still requires this step; allocation is not pre-approval.
- **Financial Authority** — who actually holds Authorization for a given expense, per Section 4's table.

The most common failure this distinction is meant to prevent: a Committee Chair assuming that because their committee "has budget," any purchase within that number is automatically approved. It isn't — every purchase still moves through the Financial Workflow in Section 5.1.

> **Leadership Decision Required: Committee Spending Authority.**
> This handbook does not set a specific amount a Committee Chair may authorize independently, if any, before Treasurer Review is required. Until decided, all committee spending — regardless of size — follows the full Financial Workflow in Section 5.1.

**5.3 — Conference Operations**

Conference-related financial planning covers registration, hotels, flights, transportation, meals, and conference clothing, coordinated with the Parent Chapter and tracked against the financial deadlines the National Collegiate 100 Policy Manual sets for Annual Conference attendance (Chapter 2).

> **Leadership Decision Required: Conference Payment Responsibilities.**
> This handbook does not specify what portion of conference costs — registration, travel, lodging, meals — the chapter covers versus what individual members are responsible for. This will reasonably vary based on the chapter's financial resources in a given year, and should be decided and communicated before each conference cycle rather than assumed to be consistent year to year. Whatever the split, it should be documented and communicated through the reporting channels in Chapter 5, not left to informal expectation.

**5.4 — Fundraising**

Every fundraiser moves through the same lifecycle as any other chapter initiative under Chapter 6 — a fundraiser is typically a Committee or Executive Initiative in that chapter's classification — with financial-specific steps layered in: Planning, Approval (per Section 5.1), Execution, cash and digital payment handling, reporting, review, and knowledge preservation.

The National Collegiate 100 Policy Manual already establishes that fundraiser tracking includes Cash App and Double Good specifically — this is Current Practice, not an open decision, and this chapter treats those two channels as already-sanctioned rather than flagging them as undecided.

> **Leadership Decision Required: Cash Handling Procedures.**
> This handbook does not specify the physical process for handling cash collected during a fundraiser — counting procedures, dual-custody expectations, deposit timelines. Given that cash is the highest-risk payment type for the discrepancy and documentation risks named in Section 8, this is a decision worth prioritizing rather than leaving informal.

> **Leadership Decision Required: Additional Digital Payment Platforms.**
> Beyond the already-established Cash App and Double Good, whether the chapter adopts additional digital payment platforms (Venmo, Zelle, or others) is undecided. Any platform adopted should route through the same Recordkeeping and Oversight structure in Section 4 as existing channels — this framework does not need to change to accommodate a new platform, only the list of sanctioned channels does.

**5.5 — Separation of Duties**

This is one of the most important sections in this chapter, and the principle behind it should govern financial decisions this handbook hasn't specifically anticipated.

Financial responsibility is distributed across the four categories in Section 4 — Authorization, Custody, Recordkeeping, and Oversight — for a specific reason: when one person holds all four for a given transaction, there is no structural check against error or misuse, regardless of that person's character. This is not a statement about anyone's trustworthiness. It's a statement about what a resilient system looks like independent of who's currently running it — the same standard Chapter 1 sets for every other system in this handbook, applied here to money specifically because money is where a single point of failure is most costly.

Scalable principles, not permanent assignments:

- The person who authorizes a purchase should not be the same person who reconciles the record of it.
- Custody of funds and the recordkeeping of those funds should be held by different people wherever chapter size allows it.
- Oversight of a transaction should sit with someone who holds none of the other three responsibilities for that specific transaction.
- As the chapter grows, these principles get easier to apply cleanly — the framework should not need to change, only the number of people available to fill each role.
- As the chapter shrinks, some overlap may become unavoidable — when it does, Oversight becomes even more important, not less, since it's the check most able to survive a smaller officer pool.

Do not assign Authorization, Custody, Recordkeeping, and Oversight permanently to one individual simply because that individual is capable. Capability is not the same as structural resilience, and this section exists specifically because the two get confused.

**5.6 — Financial Records**

This directly extends Chapter 5's Information Ownership model to financial-specific records:

| Record | Owner | Updates | Access | Approves Changes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Budgets | Treasurer | Treasurer, with Committee Chair/Project Owner input | Executive Board | President |
| Receipts | Treasurer | Whoever incurred the expense, submitted to Treasurer | Treasurer, President | N/A — accurate submission only |
| Expense Reports | Treasurer | Treasurer | Executive Board | President |
| Donation Records | Treasurer | Treasurer | Executive Board, Advisor | Treasurer |
| Fundraiser Reports | Treasurer, with Project Owner input per Chapter 6 | Treasurer | Executive Board | Treasurer |
| Conference Records | Treasurer | Treasurer | Executive Board, Advisor | President |
| Financial Summaries | Treasurer | Treasurer, per Chapter 5's monthly Reporting cadence | Executive Board | President |
| Historical Financial Records | Treasurer (current), Historian (archival) | Treasurer | Executive Board, incoming officers | Treasurer |
| Officer Transition Documents (financial) | Outgoing Treasurer | Outgoing Treasurer, reviewed with incoming Treasurer | President, incoming Treasurer | President |

Financial records are among the highest-consequence knowledge this handbook governs — a Treasurer transition without a complete handoff of the records above is a direct violation of Chapter 1's principle that institutional knowledge belongs to the chapter, not to individual officers.

**5.7 — Financial Calendar**

This section is rhythm, not policy — it does not set dates or dollar amounts, only the recurring checkpoints a future Treasurer should expect to hit every semester, synced with the Executive Calendar Chapter 3 already establishes.

```
Beginning of Semester
        ↓
Committee Budgets Submitted  (ties to Chapter 3's Semester Planning)
        ↓
Fundraising Schedule Set
        ↓
Mid-Semester Financial Review
        ↓
Conference Planning Financial Checkpoint  (timed to National Conference
                                            deadlines, per Chapter 2)
        ↓
End-of-Semester Financial Review  (feeds Section 10's Continuous Improvement)
        ↓
Treasurer Transition  (ties to Chapter 3's Transition Planning)
```

Every other cadence in this handbook — the Executive Calendar (Chapter 3), Committee meeting rhythm (Chapter 4), the Reporting System (Chapter 5) — has an established rhythm of its own. Finance did not, until this section: a system that only reacts to requests as they arrive, with no predictable checkpoints of its own, is harder for a future Treasurer to plan around and harder for the rest of the Executive Board to anticipate. This calendar doesn't require chapter leadership to have decided any of the open items in this chapter's Leadership Decisions Required list — it only requires knowing when, each semester, those decisions and their downstream effects should be revisited.

### 6. Standard Practices

- Receipts are submitted within a consistent window of the purchase — a future Executive Board should set this window explicitly rather than leaving it informal, given how directly late documentation feeds Section 8's risks.
- Financial reporting follows Chapter 5's Reporting System cadence — the Treasurer Financial Summary is monthly, per Chapter 3.
- Reconciliation between Recordkeeping and actual funds happens on a predictable cadence, not only when a discrepancy is already suspected.
- Every budget request includes the specific field from Chapter 6's Event Planning Framework it originated from, when applicable, so financial planning and initiative planning stay connected rather than duplicated.
- Financial communication — deadlines, reminders, report distribution — follows Chapter 5's channel guidance exactly, with no separate financial-specific communication rules.

### 7. Success Metrics

- **Financial Planning Accuracy** — not simply whether spending stayed within budget, but whether the original estimate was realistic in the first place. A committee that consistently requests significantly more than it actually spends is planning poorly in the opposite direction from overspending — the request itself was inaccurate, even though nothing was technically overspent. Tying up a $500 allocation for an initiative that only needed $150 makes the chapter's overall financial picture harder to forecast and can crowd out other committees' requests during Treasurer Review. This is tracked as its own signal, distinct from whether spending stayed within whatever was requested.
- **Reporting timeliness** — whether the Treasurer Financial Summary and other reports in Section 5.6 are actually submitted on the cadence Chapter 5 establishes.
- **Reconciliation completeness** — whether Recordkeeping matches actual funds consistently, not just at year-end.
- **Transparency** — whether Executive Board members can actually answer basic financial questions about the chapter without having to specially request information, consistent with Section 2's trust principle.
- **Audit-readiness** — whether the records in Section 5.6 could survive a review by the Parent Chapter or National at any point, not only when one is scheduled.
- **Separation of duties adherence** — whether Section 5.5's principles are actually being followed in practice, not just stated as policy.
- **Officer transition completeness** — whether an incoming Treasurer receives a complete handoff per Section 5.6, assessed as part of Chapter 3's Transition Planning.

### 8. Risks & Common Failure Points

- **Missing receipts.** The most common documentation failure, and the one most likely to compound — a missing receipt this semester makes next semester's reconciliation harder even if nothing else goes wrong.
- **Unauthorized purchases.** Spending that bypasses Section 5.1's workflow entirely, often from a good-faith assumption that budget allocation alone was sufficient authorization — see Section 5.2's explicit correction of that assumption.
- **Poor documentation.** A transaction that's recorded incompletely is nearly as risky as one that's not recorded at all, since it creates the appearance of a paper trail without the substance of one.
- **Overspending.** Treating a Budget Allocation as a spending target rather than a planning tool, contrary to Section 2's principle.
- **Cash discrepancies.** The specific risk Section 5.4's Cash Handling Procedures decision is meant to address — until that decision is made, cash-heavy fundraisers carry more risk than digital-payment initiatives by default.
- **Late reimbursements.** Both a member-experience problem and a documentation risk — a reimbursement submitted long after the purchase is harder to verify and more likely to be forgotten entirely.
- **Poor forecasting.** Budget requests built on optimistic guesses rather than the actual costs from Chapter 6's After Action Review data, compounding inaccuracy year over year instead of improving it.
- **Officer transition failures.** A financial handoff that doesn't follow Section 5.6's ownership table in full — this is a direct, specific instance of Chapter 3's broader succession-planning risk, applied to the role where a gap is most costly.
- **Lack of transparency.** Financial information that's technically recorded but practically inaccessible to the people in Section 4 who are supposed to have access — a records system that satisfies the letter of Section 5.6 without satisfying its purpose.
- **Single points of financial failure.** The risk Section 5.5 exists to prevent structurally — worth naming here directly as its own risk, since a chapter can read Section 5.5 and still default back to convenience over resilience without noticing.
- **Leadership Decisions left unresolved indefinitely.** Worth naming as its own risk, not just a footnote: every Leadership Decision Required callout in this chapter is a real gap in the financial system until chapter leadership actually decides it. A chapter that reads this handbook once and never returns to close those decisions has not actually finished building its financial system — it has only documented where the gaps are.

### 9. Future Platform Opportunities

Consistent with this handbook's standing rule, automation here should reduce administrative work while preserving financial accountability:

- **Member financial ledger** — tracking dues and individual financial obligations per member.
- **Committee budgets** — structured allocation tracking supporting Section 5.2.
- **Budget dashboards** — a live view of allocation versus actual spending, supporting Section 7's accuracy metric.
- **Expense requests** — structured intake feeding Section 5.1's workflow.
- **Receipt uploads** — reducing the friction behind Section 8's most common risk.
- **Fundraiser tracking** — supporting Section 5.4, including the already-sanctioned Cash App and Double Good channels.
- **Conference payment tracking** — supporting Section 5.3 once payment responsibilities are decided.
- **Donation tracking** — structured recordkeeping for Section 5.6.
- **Approval workflows** — routing per Section 5.1's steps, once thresholds are set.
- **Financial reports** — templated generation feeding Chapter 5's Reporting System.
- **Digital archives** — the system behind Section 5.6's records table.
- **Officer dashboards** — a Treasurer-specific view supporting Chapter 3's Executive dashboards.

None of the above should extend into authorizing a purchase, setting a policy, or making a financial judgment call — those remain human decisions, consistent with the boundary held throughout this handbook, and especially important here given the accountability stakes Section 5.5 establishes.

### 10. Continuous Improvement

Each semester, the financial system should improve through:

- **Semester financial reviews**, evaluating the aggregate of the period's Record Archive against the Budget Allocations set at the start of the semester, timed to the Mid-Semester and End-of-Semester checkpoints in Section 5.7's Financial Calendar.
- **Officer feedback**, particularly from the Treasurer and anyone who submitted a budget request, on whether Section 5.1's workflow is actually working.
- **Treasurer transition**, using Section 5.6's ownership table as the explicit handoff checklist, not an informal conversation.
- **Budget improvements**, using actual spending data to build more accurate forecasts, addressing the poor-forecasting risk in Section 8.
- **Fundraiser analysis**, reviewing what actually worked against what was planned, per Chapter 6's After Action Review model.
- **Knowledge preservation**, ensuring financial lessons — not just financial records — carry forward, consistent with Chapter 5's Organizational Memory practices.
- **Platform enhancements**, evaluated against Section 9's boundary.
- **Transparency improvements**, checking whether Section 7's transparency metric is actually improving or just being restated each year.

## Leadership Decisions Required — Consolidated

This chapter identifies eight specific decisions chapter leadership has not yet made. They are collected here as a single roadmap, in addition to appearing inline where each is most relevant:

1. **Independent Chapter Banking** (Section 4) — whether and when to move from the Current State to the Future State custody model.
2. **Spending Approval Thresholds** (Section 5.1) — the dollar line separating routine from escalated review.
3. **Emergency Expenditures** (Section 5.1) — what qualifies, and who may authorize spending under time pressure.
4. **Committee Spending Authority** (Section 5.2) — whether Committee Chairs may authorize any spending independently before Treasurer Review.
5. **Conference Payment Responsibilities** (Section 5.3) — the chapter-versus-member cost split for conference attendance.
6. **Cash Handling Procedures** (Section 5.4) — the physical process for counting, custody, and deposit of fundraiser cash.
7. **Additional Digital Payment Platforms** (Section 5.4) — whether to adopt channels beyond the already-sanctioned Cash App and Double Good.
8. **Debit Card Custody** — if the chapter obtains a debit card under either the Current or Future State, who holds it, and under what authorization it may be used, has not been addressed anywhere in this chapter and should be resolved as part of whichever custody decision (Item 1) it ends up depending on.

This list should be reviewed at the start of each academic year, alongside the rest of this handbook's Continuous Improvement practices — a decision made this year does not need to stay open next year, and a decision this year's Executive Board doesn't reach shouldn't quietly disappear from view either.
